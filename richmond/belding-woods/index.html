The two price numbers that disagree, and why

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions, and it is worth saying why rather than quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Belding Woods is down 1.3 percent year over year. The median asking price of what is actively listed in Richmond is n/a year over year, and price per square foot is n/a.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

The part that costs people the most money over time is the mortgage insurance rule, not the rate. On FHA with less than 10 percent down, the annual mortgage insurance premium stays on the loan for as long as the loan exists. The only ways off it are a refinance or a sale. On conventional, private mortgage insurance comes off at 80 percent loan to value, either automatically or on request with a new valuation. Two loans can quote nearly the same payment in month one and separate by a large number over the years that follow, purely on that one rule.

Private mortgage insurance has no published rate card. It is priced per borrower on credit score and loan to value, and it runs roughly 0.3 percent to 1.5 percent of the loan per year. Any article that quotes you a single PMI number is guessing. The real figure comes from a quote with your own credit profile on it.

NACA sits outside both of those. The Neighborhood Assistance Corporation of America program carries no down payment requirement, no mortgage insurance, no lender closing costs, a below-market fixed rate, and no minimum credit score. The trade is process. There is required counselling, a membership requirement, a documentation standard that is stricter than a normal file, and a timeline measured in months rather than weeks. For buyers whose obstacle is cash rather than income, it is frequently the strongest program available, and it is the one most often left out of the conversation. Terms and the current rate come from NACA directly at naca.com, which is why this article links there instead of quoting a rate that will be stale next month.

First-time buyer programs that reach Richmond

The statewide programs come from CalHFA, and they stack with a first mortgage rather than replacing it. The constraint that actually binds in a neighborhood at $509,894 is almost never income. It is the program's own purchase price cap, which is frequently set below the county loan limit.

CalHFA MyHome Assistance. A deferred-payment junior loan toward the down payment and closing costs, paired with a CalHFA first mortgage. Available year round. Nothing is repaid until the home is sold or refinanced or the first loan is paid off.

CalHFA ZIP. Covers closing costs on a CalHFA conventional first mortgage, and can be combined with MyHome.

CalHFA Dream For All. A shared-appreciation second loan, limited to first-generation buyers, meaning the buyer's parents have not owned a home. It runs in announced application windows rather than continuously, and the appreciation share is repaid on sale or refinance. Whether it is open depends on whether a round is currently running, which is published at calhfa.ca.gov.

GSFA Platinum. A separate down payment assistance program, structured as a grant or a silent second depending on the option chosen, with its own income and price limits.

NACA. No down payment, no mortgage insurance, no lender closing costs, a below-market fixed rate, no minimum credit score, in exchange for a counselling and documentation process measured in months. For a buyer whose constraint is cash rather than income, this is frequently the strongest option on the list.

City and county programs. Richmond and Contra Costa County administer their own first-time buyer and below-market-rate programs, with their own eligibility and their own price caps, and they change with funding cycles. These are the ones most often missed, because they are not listed anywhere alongside the state programs.

A detail that catches people in Richmond: most down payment assistance programs carry their own purchase price caps, and those caps are often lower than the county loan limit. A program can be open to you on income and still not reach the typical home in a neighborhood like Belding Woods. The order of operations that works is to establish the price the program allows first, then shop inside it, rather than finding a home and asking afterward whether assistance can stretch to cover it.

Property tax, and the three rules that decide your bill

On a home at $509,894 in Belding Woods, property tax runs about $557 a month, or $6,680 a year, at the effective rate used throughout this report. Three rules decide what any given owner here actually pays, and they produce very different bills on identical houses.

Proposition 13 sets the base at 1 percent of assessed value and caps the growth of that assessed value at 2 percent per year while one owner holds the property. Voter-approved bonds and special assessments sit on top of the 1 percent base, which is why the real rate runs above 1 percent almost everywhere in California. The effective rate used in this article is 1.31 percent, the Contra Costa County effective rate, sourced to Ownwell property tax trends.

The rule that surprises buyers is reassessment. A long-time owner next door can pay a fraction of what a new buyer pays on an identical house, because their assessed value has been climbing at 2 percent a year from a purchase decades ago while a new purchase resets assessed value to the price paid. Any property tax figure you read that was calculated from median taxes actually paid will understate a new purchase substantially, because it is averaging in those protected assessments. This article uses the new-buyer basis instead.

The second rule is the supplemental bill. A purchase triggers a reassessment, and the difference between the seller's old assessed value and the new one arrives as a separate supplemental tax bill, often months after closing and often after the escrow impound account was already set up on the old number. It is a real and routine shortfall in the first year of ownership.

The third rule is Proposition 19. Since 2021 it has governed two things that matter here. A homeowner who is 55 or older, or severely disabled, or a wildfire or disaster victim, can transfer their existing assessed value to a replacement home anywhere in California, up to three times, with an upward adjustment if the replacement costs more. That is the rule that makes a move financially possible for long-time owners who would otherwise be locked in place by their own tax basis. Proposition 19 also narrowed the parent to child exclusion sharply. An inherited home now keeps the parent's assessed value only if a child makes it their own primary residence and files on time, and even then only up to a value cap, with the excess reassessed. Inherited property that becomes a rental or a second home gets reassessed to market value. Families who assumed the old rules still applied are the ones who get a tax bill they did not plan for.

ADUs, and what they are actually worth here

At $509,894 typical value and $509 per square foot in Richmond, added permitted square footage is the most direct equity lever available to an owner here, and an accessory dwelling unit is the version of that with its own rules.

California law now requires local agencies to allow accessory dwelling units on residential lots, with ministerial approval and a decision deadline, and it has steadily removed the obstacles cities used to rely on. Owner-occupancy requirements on new ADUs have been eliminated, which means both the main house and the ADU can be rented. Impact fees are waived for smaller units. Size limits are calculated on interior livable space.

The provision with the largest equity consequence is Assembly Bill 1033, which allows a city to let an ADU be sold separately from the main house as its own condominium unit. The important detail, and the one that most coverage gets wrong, is that AB 1033 is permissive rather than mandatory. The state allows cities to opt in. It does not require them to. A relatively small number of California cities have adopted an enabling ordinance so far, and in a city that has not adopted one, the statute gives an owner no right to sell an ADU separately. Whether it applies to a given property is a question for that city's planning department and its municipal code, not a question that can be answered from the state law alone. Where it has been adopted, the process is demanding: a condominium plan, compliance with subdivision law, a safety inspection before recording, and consent from every existing lienholder, which is the step that stops most attempts.

The practical valuation point for an owner here is simpler. An ADU adds value in two ways that behave very differently. Rental income supports value through the income it produces, and appraisers in this market treat that unevenly depending on whether the unit is permitted and legally rentable. Permitted square footage adds value more directly and more reliably. Unpermitted square footage frequently adds the least value of all, because it introduces a disclosure obligation, a lender problem, and a negotiating lever for the buyer. The gap between a permitted conversion and an identical unpermitted one is the single largest avoidable value difference in this category.

Selling a home in a divorce

There is a point in most settlements where the house stops being a home and becomes the hardest line item in the agreement. At $509,894 typical value, with a monthly carry around $5,225 and a refinance market at 7.4 percent, the arithmetic in Belding Woods is unforgiving in a specific way: a buyout that looked affordable at the rate on the existing loan frequently does not survive underwriting at today's rate on one income.

What I see as the listing agent, repeatedly:

The house is usually the largest asset and the only one that cannot be divided without either a sale or a buyout, which makes it the piece that sets the pace of the whole settlement.

Timing and authority are frequently unresolved. Listing a jointly held property generally requires both signatures, and a listing agreement signed by one spouse without the other creates a problem that surfaces at the worst moment.

A buyout has to clear a lender. Removing one spouse from the loan is a refinance, and it is underwritten on the remaining spouse's income alone, at today's rate rather than the rate on the existing loan. A buyout that pencils on a spreadsheet often does not survive that underwriting at current rates.

Occupancy during the listing period affects how the home shows and how disclosures get handled, and it is usually the most emotionally loaded logistics question in the file.

Deferred maintenance tends to accumulate during the period when neither party wants to spend money on a house they are leaving.

What I do not do:

I do not give legal advice, draft or interpret the marital settlement agreement, or advise on how the proceeds should be characterised or divided. That belongs with the family law attorney.

I do not give tax advice on the capital gains exclusion, the timing of the sale relative to the filing status, or the basis consequences of a buyout. That belongs with the CPA.

I do not take sides, carry messages between the parties, or choose which party's timeline wins.

What I do:

I provide a written valuation and net-proceeds analysis that both counsel can drop into their own models, with the holding-cost and net-sheet math shown rather than asserted.

I flag the real estate issues early, including the title vesting, the refinance feasibility question, the supplemental tax exposure, and any permit or disclosure problem that will surface in escrow anyway.

I run the listing and the escrow so that neither party has to coordinate with the other to get it done, and I report to both sides and both counsel equally.

I work to the court's or the agreement's timeline rather than arguing with it.

The pattern that produces the best financial outcome is boring: the valuation and the holding-cost math get done before the settlement terms are fixed, not after. A settlement that commits to a sale timeline without knowing the monthly carry has committed to a number nobody calculated.

Selling a home in probate or from a trust

The most expensive thing an estate can do with a house in Belding Woods is nothing, for several months, while the family decides. At $5,225 a month in carry, that is $31,352 over half a year, and the statutory fee structure does not shrink while they wait.

The structural fact that drives everything in California is that statutory probate fees are calculated on the gross value of the estate's assets, not on the family's equity. California Probate Code section 10810 sets the attorney fee schedule, and section 10800 applies the same schedule to the personal representative. The schedule runs 4 percent on the first 100,000 dollars of gross value, 3 percent on the next 100,000, 2 percent on the next 800,000, and 1 percent on the next 9 million. The mortgage does not reduce the calculation. A home worth a great deal with very little equity still generates fees on the full value.

Run it on this neighborhood. A home at the typical $509,894 generates a statutory attorney fee of about $13,198 under that schedule, and the personal representative is entitled to the same amount under section 10800, for a combined statutory cost of roughly $26,396 before court filing fees, probate referee fees, publication costs or any of the secondary expenses. If the home carries a large mortgage, that figure does not move. It is calculated on the $509,894, not on what is left after the bank.

Assembly Bill 2016 changed the picture for smaller estates. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, the revised Probate Code section 13150 allows a streamlined petition to transfer a decedent's primary residence valued up to 750,000 dollars without a full probate, which avoids the statutory fee schedule entirely. The threshold applies to the primary residence only, and non-residential real property remains subject to a far lower threshold under section 13200. {{PROBATE_THRESHOLD_NOTE}}

I do not give legal advice, interpret the will or the trust, advise on whether probate is required, or opine on a personal representative's or trustee's duties. That belongs with the probate attorney.

I do not advise on the estate tax, the basis step-up under Internal Revenue Code section 1014, or the income tax consequences of a sale by the estate. That belongs with the CPA.

I do not take instructions from one beneficiary over the others.

What I do:

I provide the date-of-death valuation support and the current market valuation the attorney and the CPA both need, with comparable sales documented.

I flag the real estate issues that change the timeline, including the title vesting, any lien or recording problem, the condition and vacancy questions, and the insurance exposure on a vacant property, which is the one most estates miss.

I carry the property through a sale that meets the court's confirmation requirements where those apply, and I keep the personal representative out of the day-to-day coordination.

I give the family the holding-cost number, so that the choice between selling now and holding through a longer process is a decision made against a real monthly figure rather than a feeling.

The expensive pattern is always the same. An estate holds a vacant property for months while the family decides, insurance on a vacant home is quietly more expensive or quietly lapsed, the carry runs every month, and the eventual sale price is no better than it would have been at the start.

What are property taxes in Richmond?

I use 1.31 percent in this report, the Contra Costa County effective rate. On a home at $509,894 that is roughly $557 a month, $6,680 a year. Proposition 13 sets the base at 1 percent of assessed value and caps growth in that assessed value at 2 percent a year while one owner holds the property, and voter-approved bonds sit on top, which is why the real rate runs above 1 percent. The part that catches buyers is that a purchase resets assessed value to the price paid. Any figure you read that was built from median taxes actually paid is averaging in decades of protected assessments and will understate what a new buyer owes.

What is the transfer tax in Richmond?

This is the cost people forget to budget for, and it varies more by city line than anything else in a California transaction. Richmond is a chartered city, so the rate here is $8.10 per $1,000 of value. On a home at $509,894 that is $4,130. For scale, the identical sale price would carry $561 in Antioch and $6,680 in El Cerrito, both in this same county. There is also a cliff worth knowing about. The rate applies to the whole value, not just the portion above a threshold, so crossing $1,000,000 reprices the entire sale. At exactly $1,000,000 the tax is $8,100. One dollar higher it is $13,500. That is $5,400 for one dollar of price. The typical home here is $490,106 below that line, which makes it a live pricing question rather than a theoretical one. Who pays is set by the contract and by local custom rather than by statute, and it is negotiable. Escrow is the authority on the exact figure for a specific sale.

What first-time buyer help is available in Richmond?

CalHFA runs the statewide programs and they layer on top of a first mortgage rather than replacing it. MyHome is a deferred-payment junior loan toward the down payment and closing costs, open year round. ZIP covers closing costs on a CalHFA conventional first. Dream For All is the shared-appreciation program, limited to first-generation buyers, and it runs in announced windows rather than continuously. GSFA Platinum is a separate option, and Richmond and Contra Costa County run their own programs with their own funding cycles, which are the ones most people never hear about. The constraint that actually binds in a neighborhood at $509,894 is almost never income. It is the program's own purchase price cap, which is often set below the county loan limit. I would establish the price a program allows first, then shop inside it. Doing it the other way around is how people fall for a house the assistance cannot reach.

Can I build an ADU in Richmond?

Almost certainly yes, in some form. State law requires local agencies to allow accessory dwelling units on residential lots with ministerial approval and a decision deadline, so the real question is what size and configuration the local ordinance permits, not whether you are allowed. Owner-occupancy requirements on new ADUs have been removed, so the main house and the ADU can both be rented. Selling one separately is a different question. Assembly Bill 1033 lets a city allow an ADU to be sold as its own condominium unit, but it is permissive rather than mandatory, relatively few cities have adopted an enabling ordinance, and without one there is no right to do it. That is a question for the Richmond planning department and the municipal code, not something the state law answers on its own.

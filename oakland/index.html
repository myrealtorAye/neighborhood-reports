What Oakland actually is

Oakland's chain of title starts with one land grant. In 1820 the last Spanish governor of California gave Sergeant Luis Maria Peralta about 44,800 acres covering what is now Oakland, Berkeley, Alameda, Emeryville, Piedmont and San Leandro. The United States took California, the Land Act of 1851 made the family prove the grant in court, and by the time the Supreme Court confirmed it in 1856 squatters held most of it. Six acres survived as a park.

Then the city ate its neighbours. In 1897 it annexed Temescal, Golden Gate, Bushrod and the Piedmont Avenue area. In 1909 it took Fruitvale, Elmhurst, Melrose and Fitchburg in the largest annexation it ever made, carrying the city from 22.9 square miles to roughly 60 while the population doubled to 150,174 by 1910, most of that from people who left San Francisco after the 1906 earthquake.

A few things here are genuinely first in the country. The state legislature protected Lake Merritt in 1870, making it the first wildlife refuge in North America. Frederick Law Olmsted, who designed Central Park, laid out Mountain View Cemetery in 1863. The 16th Street Station opened in 1912 as the western terminus of the transcontinental railroad and became the western headquarters of the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters.

The hills tell a different story. The San Antonio redwood forest was big enough that ships entering San Francisco Bay steered by two of its trees to clear Blossom Rock, and it was logged level inside about fifteen years. On October 20, 1991 the Tunnel Fire killed 25 people and destroyed 2,843 single family homes across those same hills.

What all of that produces is a city of 191,713 housing units where 46.6 percent predate 1950. Twenty miles south in Fremont that figure is 2 percent. Only 41.5 percent of Oakland units are single family detached and owner occupancy is 42.3 percent, so this is substantially a flats and multi unit market. Zillow tracks 121 separate neighborhoods inside the city.

So Oakland is not one market, it is three. Pre war flats, where the questions are the legal unit count, the rent rules and a long inspection list. The hills, where fire insurance decides affordability before the house does. And downtown, where it is reserve studies and seismic retrofit.

Find out which one you are shopping in before you compare a single price, and pull the year built before anything else.

Downtown and Lake Merritt

Downtown

Downtown Oakland's commercial centre was fixed at Ninth Street and Broadway by 1877, and Broadway has been the spine ever since.

The district grew along the 7th Street railroad line in the 1870s, and the oldest surviving piece of it is the six blocks now called Old Oakland, between Broadway, Jefferson, 7th and 10th, where the Victorians from the 1870s and 1880s are still standing. The Nicholl Block dates to 1878. As the city expanded northward the old core was left behind, went economically depressed, and sat with vacant buildings for decades, and that neglect is the reason the nineteenth century architecture survived at all. Restoration of 9th Street between Washington and Broadway in the 1970s and 1980s produced the block called Victorian Row.

What downtown is now is the densest part of Oakland: office buildings, two BART stations, civic buildings, and residential towers and converted lofts among the older stock.

Almost nothing here is a single family house. Oakland has 191,713 housing units and only 41.5 percent are single family detached, with owner occupancy at 42.3 percent citywide, and downtown sits far below both. 46.6 percent of the city's housing predates 1950.

So this is a condominium and loft purchase, and the building matters more than the unit.

Get the association budget and the reserve study, not the monthly dues, because the reserve position is what becomes a special assessment. Ask what the association insures and what you insure. Ask whether the building has had a seismic retrofit and whether it is classified soft story or unreinforced masonry, since downtown has a lot of both and the answer affects financing, insurance and your own exposure.

Ask what is entitled on the parcels around you. Downtown is where Oakland approves height, and a view is not a protected asset.

Oakland's transfer tax runs 16.10 dollars per thousand at the citywide typical value, and the bracket applies to the whole price, so check your number against the next threshold before you set it.

Then stand on the street at nine on a weeknight. Downtown empties, and you should know what that version feels like.

Old Oakland

This is where Oakland actually started, and the Victorians are still standing because the city moved on and left them alone.

Old Oakland is six blocks bounded by Broadway, Jefferson, 7th and 10th Streets. The commercial district grew along the 7th Street railroad line in the 1870s, and by 1877 the hub was established at Ninth Street and Broadway. Most of the buildings you see went up in the 1870s and 1880s, among them the Nicholl Block from 1878.

Then the city grew uptown and this district stopped being the centre. It became economically depressed and a lot of the buildings sat vacant for decades. That neglect is exactly why they survived. Nobody had the money or the reason to tear them down and put up something taller. In the 1970s and 1980s developers restored the block of 9th Street between Washington and Broadway, and it has been called Victorian Row since.

So what exists here is a concentrated run of nineteenth century commercial architecture in a downtown. That is rare in California, because most cities replaced theirs.

The residential inventory is almost all lofts and condominiums converted out of or built among those commercial buildings. Oakland has 191,713 housing units with 46.6 percent predating 1950, and in this district the share is near total.

What to do about that. On a converted loft or a condominium in an older building, the first question is the building, not the unit. Get the association budget and the reserve study. Ask what the association insures and what falls to you. Ask directly whether the building has had a seismic retrofit and whether it is classified soft story, because unreinforced masonry and soft story construction both affect financing, insurance and your own risk, and a nineteenth century commercial building is a candidate for both.

Ask about the mechanical systems too. Conversions put residential plumbing, electrical and HVAC into shells never designed for it, and the quality varies enormously.

Then walk 9th Street between Washington and Broadway on a Friday evening. That block is the argument for living here.

Chinatown

Typical home value $334,594, down 0.66 percent month over month, down 5.6 percent year over year. Full Chinatown report.

Oakland Chinatown is older than almost everything around it. By 1860 there were about 200 Chinese residents in a city of roughly 1,500 people.

The community settled around 8th and Webster Streets and built a business centre there that is still the centre today. The neighborhood is one of the oldest continuously occupied Chinatowns in the country and it never became primarily a tourist district. That is the usual fate. It is a working commercial neighborhood with grocers, herbalists, restaurants and family associations.

The twentieth century cost it a great deal of ground. The Laney College and Chinatown redevelopment plan and the construction of BART in the early 1970s cut through the neighborhood. One local history puts the displacement at around 10,000 residents, and the losses included the Ming Quong Home, an orphanage for girls at 51 Ninth Street. Redevelopment of that era is the single biggest reason the street pattern here is interrupted the way it is.

The housing is dense and old. Oakland has 191,713 housing units with 46.6 percent built before 1950, and in Chinatown the stock skews older and overwhelmingly multi unit. Owner occupancy citywide is 42.3 percent and in this part of the city it runs far lower.

So this is an income property and condominium market more than a single family one, and the rules are the first thing to learn.

Confirm the legal unit count with the city on any building, and read Oakland's rent adjustment program and just cause for eviction rules before you model income, because they apply to most of this stock and they are not optional. Ask whether the building is unreinforced masonry or soft story, since both are common in pre 1950 commercial and mixed use buildings here and both affect financing and insurance.

Get the permit history, and on a mixed use building ask the city what the ground floor is legally permitted to be.

Then spend a Saturday morning at the 8th and Webster markets. The commercial density is the asset here and it does not read in photographs.

Jack London

Jack London drank in a bar down here that is still open, and he put it in his books seventeen times.

Johnny Heinold opened the First and Last Chance Saloon on this waterfront in 1883, though one city history gives the date as June 1, 1884. London was a local kid and the saloon became his base. It is the setting behind scenes in The Call of the Wild and The Sea Wolf, it gets referenced seventeen times in John Barleycorn, and it has been nicknamed Jack London's Rendezvous ever since. The floor still slopes from the 1906 earthquake.

Everything else about this district follows from the water. It was working port and warehouse ground, and the Amtrak station is here. That makes it the one Oakland neighborhood where you can walk from your door to a long distance train.

The housing is almost entirely converted warehouses and newer construction: lofts, condominiums and apartment buildings put into or built beside industrial shells. That is unusual for Oakland, where 46.6 percent of the 191,713 housing units predate 1950 and most of the older stock is houses instead of warehouses.

So shop it as a loft and condominium market with industrial neighbours, and ask the industrial questions.

Get the association budget and the reserve study, not the dues figure, and ask what the association insures. Ask whether the building has had a seismic retrofit and whether it is soft story or unreinforced masonry, because converted industrial buildings are frequently both and it changes your financing.

Ask the city what the surrounding parcels permit, because this is a working port district and the use next door can change in ways a residential neighborhood's cannot. Then ask about noise and about freight and train movement at the specific address, at night.

And on anything near the estuary, get the flood zone determination with a written insurance quote before your contingencies expire.

Then go sit in Heinold's for twenty minutes. The building is a listed piece of Oakland and it costs you the price of one drink to understand the neighborhood's whole history.

Lake Merritt

Typical home value $674,272, up 0.68 percent month over month, down 0.1 percent year over year. Full Lake Merritt report.

In 1870 the California legislature made this lake the first wildlife refuge in North America. Nothing else in the country had that protection yet.

Samuel Merritt had proposed it the year before. One account puts the official designation at March 18, 1870. Worth knowing: the 1870 law did not actually use the words wildlife refuge, so the protection started out looser than the anniversary plaques suggest. What is not in dispute is that the lake sits on the Pacific Flyway, the main north and south route for birds along the west coast, and that is why the protection mattered and still does.

The other thing on the shore is stranger and more consequential. Children's Fairyland opened here in 1950, a storybook theme park conceived by a local business owner named Arthur Navlet. Walt Disney came to look at it while he was working out his own park, and Disneyland opened five years later. The small park on the north shore of a lake in Oakland is part of the ancestry of the whole modern theme park.

The neighborhood around the lake is the densest residential ground in Oakland: apartment buildings and condominiums of every era, from 1920s courtyard buildings to recent towers, wrapped around a tidal lagoon with a three mile path on it.

Oakland has 191,713 housing units and 46.6 percent of them predate 1950. The newer cities in this county are the reverse of that. Around Lake Merritt that share runs higher still.

So the questions here are the multi unit and pre war questions. On a condominium, get the association budget and the reserve study, not the dues number, ask what the association insures and what you must, and ask directly about any seismic retrofit history, because lenders ask about soft story construction on older buildings and the answer affects your financing.

On a small apartment building, confirm the legal unit count with the city and read Oakland's rent adjustment and just cause rules before you model a single dollar of income. They apply to most older buildings here and they are specific.

Then walk the full loop around the lake once. It is three miles and it is the reason people pay to be here.

Temescal

Typical home value $1,087,718, up 0.86 percent month over month, up 6.5 percent year over year. Full Temescal report.

The neighborhood is named after a sweat lodge, by way of the Aztec language, through Spanish.

A Huchiun village stood beside the creek here, and the people who lived in it built large sweat lodges on its banks. Early Spanish settlers called the structures temescalli, an Aztec word, and the name transferred to the creek and then to the district. In 1868 Anthony Chabot dammed the creek valley to create Oakland's first water supply at municipal scale, the body of water now called Lake Temescal. The creek itself was buried during the development push of the 1960s and 1970s. It still runs under the neighborhood in a culvert.

The next layer is the Colombo Club, still operating here and described as the largest private Italian social club west of the Mississippi. It is the surviving institution of the era when this district was the centre of Italian social and commercial life in Oakland, and the clubhouse and the businesses along Telegraph Avenue are what is left of that period on the ground.

Then there is Temescal Alley. Those buildings were stable blocks and carriage houses in the early 1900s, and they now hold a run of very small shops. That alley is the single most photographed thing in the neighborhood.

Oakland annexed Temescal in 1897, the same year it took Piedmont Avenue, Golden Gate and Bushrod.

The housing is pre war Oakland flats: bungalows and Craftsman houses on a grid, with apartment buildings and small income properties along the arterials. Oakland has 191,713 housing units with 71,234 built in 1939 or earlier, putting 46.6 percent of the city before 1950. Owner occupancy citywide is 42.3 percent.

So confirm the legal unit count with the city on anything that might hold more than one household, and read Oakland's rent adjustment program and the just cause for eviction rules before you model income.

Then the pre war list: foundation bolting, knob and tube wiring, galvanized supply lines, the sewer lateral Oakland certifies at sale, and the permit history on every addition.

Then walk Telegraph from 40th to 51st on a Saturday. That stretch is why people pay to be here.

Rockridge

Typical home value $1,802,087, up 0.66 percent month over month, up 10.3 percent year over year. Full Rockridge report.

In the early 1950s Frank Sinatra had trouble with his voice, and he flew a woman from Oakland out to Las Vegas to fix it. Her studio was a small dim room on College Avenue. Judy Davis taught out of that room for about fifty years, and the people who came through it include Barbra Streisand, Judy Garland, Grace Slick and Eddie Money. She got into the work the hard way: a tonsillectomy at nineteen damaged her own vocal cords and left her with a rasp, so she went and studied anatomy and built a method out of it. She was still teaching two weeks before she died in 2001, at 81.

College Avenue has always kept things like that in plain sight. Walk up it and you pass a Julia Morgan building at 5951. The congregation hired her in January 1917 on a ten thousand dollar budget, and she gave them a church with no steeple. She repeated triangles through the structure instead, and set a round window over the entrance. Morgan is the Hearst Castle architect and the first woman licensed to practice architecture in California. This is one of her quiet ones, and most people drive past it.

Half a mile west, where Broadway crosses Pleasant Valley, there is a shopping center sitting in a hole. The Bilger quarry worked that site on and off from 1870 to 1946 and was for years the largest rock quarry in Alameda County. Drive on an older street in North or West Oakland and you are driving on crushed rock that came out of that pit. The pond at the east end never drained. The Claremont Country Club owns it and uses it to irrigate the golf course.

The name is older than the quarry and it is completely literal. Rockridge sits on a shutter ridge thrown up by the Hayward Fault. The west side of the fault creeps north every time it moves, and the ridge has been shoved far enough across Temescal Creek to dam it, so the creek turns and runs north to get around the obstruction. Geologists call it a shutter ridge because it slides across the valley like the shutter on an old box camera. That is why the streets bend the way they do up here. It is also worth knowing before you waive an inspection.

Then there are the stairs. Developers cut hundreds of public staircases into these hills so people living up the slope could get down to the streetcars on the main road, and Upper Rockridge ended up with more of them than almost anywhere in the city. The Key System ran up College Avenue. Once everyone had a car the stairs stopped mattering and sat forgotten for decades. Oakland counts about 220 of them and rebuilt roughly 40 between 2001 and 2017. Several are still unmarked dirt and still officially public right of way, so you can walk them today if you know where they start.

The houses came in behind the streetcar. Rockridge Park was the first residential tract in 1909, and the bungalows and Craftsman cottages on the flats near College went up through the 1920s, with white stucco Spanish Colonial Revival mixed in among them. The two price numbers that disagree, and why

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Oakland is up 0.6 percent year over year. The median asking price of what is actively listed in Oakland is down 1.6 percent year over year, and price per square foot is down 5.1 percent.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

Property tax, and the three rules that decide your bill

On a home at $710,647 in Oakland, property tax runs about $1,013 a month, or $12,152 a year, at the effective rate used throughout this report. Three rules decide what any given owner here actually pays, and they produce very different bills on identical houses.

Proposition 13 sets the base at 1 percent of assessed value and caps the growth of that assessed value at 2 percent per year while one owner holds the property. Voter-approved bonds and special assessments sit on top of the 1 percent base. The real rate runs above 1 percent almost everywhere in California. The effective rate used in this article is 1.71 percent, the Oakland effective rate, sourced to Ownwell property tax trends.

The rule that surprises buyers is reassessment. A long-time owner next door can pay a fraction of what a new buyer pays on an identical house, because their assessed value has been climbing at 2 percent a year from a purchase decades ago while a new purchase resets assessed value to the price paid. Any property tax figure you read that was calculated from median taxes actually paid will understate a new purchase substantially, because it is averaging in those protected assessments. This article uses the new-buyer basis instead.

The second rule is the supplemental bill. A purchase triggers a reassessment, and the difference between the seller's old assessed value and the new one arrives as a separate supplemental tax bill, often months after closing and often after the escrow impound account was already set up on the old number. It is a real and routine shortfall in the first year of ownership.

The third rule is Proposition 19. Since 2021 it has governed two things that matter here. A homeowner who is 55 or older, or severely disabled, or a wildfire or disaster victim, can transfer their existing assessed value to a replacement home anywhere in California, up to three times, with an upward adjustment if the replacement costs more. That is the rule that makes a move financially possible for long-time owners who would otherwise be locked in place by their own tax basis. Proposition 19 also narrowed the parent to child exclusion sharply. An inherited home now keeps the parent's assessed value only if a child makes it their own primary residence and files on time, and even then only up to a value cap, with the excess reassessed. Inherited property that becomes a rental or a second home gets reassessed to market value. Families who assumed the old rules still applied are the ones who get a tax bill they did not plan for.

ADUs, and what they are actually worth here

At $710,647 typical value and $494 per square foot in Oakland, added permitted square footage is the most direct equity lever available to an owner here, and an accessory dwelling unit is the version of that with its own rules.

California law now requires local agencies to allow accessory dwelling units on residential lots, with ministerial approval and a decision deadline, and it has steadily removed the obstacles cities used to rely on. Owner-occupancy requirements on new ADUs have been eliminated. Both the main house and the ADU can be rented. Impact fees are waived for smaller units. Size limits are calculated on interior livable space.

The provision with the largest equity consequence is Assembly Bill 1033. It allows a city to let an ADU be sold separately from the main house as its own condominium unit. The important detail, and the one that most coverage gets wrong, is that AB 1033 is permissive and not mandatory. The state allows cities to opt in. It does not require them to. A relatively small number of California cities have adopted an enabling ordinance so far, and in a city that has not adopted one, the statute gives an owner no right to sell an ADU separately. Whether it applies to a given property is a question for that city's planning department and its municipal code, not a question that can be answered from the state law alone. Where it has been adopted, the process is demanding: a condominium plan, compliance with subdivision law, a safety inspection before recording, and consent from every existing lienholder. That last step stops most attempts.

The practical valuation point for an owner here is simpler. An ADU adds value in two ways that behave very differently. Rental income supports value through the income it produces, and appraisers in this market treat that unevenly depending on whether the unit is permitted and legally rentable. Permitted square footage adds value more directly and more reliably. Unpermitted square footage frequently adds the least value of all, because it introduces a disclosure obligation, a lender problem, and a negotiating lever for the buyer. The gap between a permitted conversion and an identical unpermitted one is the single largest avoidable value difference in this category.

Selling a home in a divorce

There is a point in most settlements where the house stops being a home and becomes the hardest line item in the agreement. At $710,647 typical value, with a monthly carry around $7,190 and a refinance market at 7.4 percent, the arithmetic in Oakland is unforgiving in a specific way: a buyout that looked affordable at the rate on the existing loan frequently does not survive underwriting at today's rate on one income.

What I see as the listing agent, repeatedly:

The house is usually the largest asset and the only one that cannot be divided without either a sale or a buyout. It sets the pace of the whole settlement.

Timing and authority are frequently unresolved. Listing a jointly held property generally requires both signatures, and a listing agreement signed by one spouse without the other creates a problem that surfaces at the worst moment.

A buyout has to clear a lender. Removing one spouse from the loan is a refinance, and it is underwritten on the remaining spouse's income alone, at today's rate and not the rate on the existing loan. A buyout that pencils on a spreadsheet often does not survive that underwriting at current rates.

Occupancy during the listing period affects how the home shows and how disclosures get handled, and it is usually the most emotionally loaded logistics question in the file.

Deferred maintenance tends to accumulate during the period when neither party wants to spend money on a house they are leaving.

Selling a home in probate or from a trust

The most expensive thing an estate can do with a house in Oakland is nothing, for several months, while the family decides. At $7,190 a month in carry, that is $43,140 over half a year, and the statutory fee structure does not shrink while they wait.

The structural fact that drives everything in California is that statutory probate fees are calculated on the gross value of the estate's assets, not on the family's equity. California Probate Code section 10810 sets the attorney fee schedule, and section 10800 applies the same schedule to the personal representative. The schedule runs 4 percent on the first 100,000 dollars of gross value, 3 percent on the next 100,000, 2 percent on the next 800,000, and 1 percent on the next 9 million. The mortgage does not reduce the calculation. A home worth a great deal with very little equity still generates fees on the full value.

Run it on this neighborhood. A home at the typical $710,647 generates a statutory attorney fee of about $17,213 under that schedule, and the personal representative is entitled to the same amount under section 10800, for a combined statutory cost of roughly $34,426 before court filing fees, probate referee fees, publication costs or any of the secondary expenses. If the home carries a large mortgage, that figure does not move. It is calculated on the $710,647, not on what is left after the bank.

Assembly Bill 2016 changed the picture for smaller estates. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, the revised Probate Code section 13150 allows a streamlined petition to transfer a decedent's primary residence valued up to 750,000 dollars without a full probate. The statutory fee schedule does not apply. The threshold applies to the primary residence only, and non-residential real property remains subject to a far lower threshold under section 13200. For Oakland specifically, this matters. The typical home here is $710,647, under the 750,000 dollar threshold, so the streamlined petition is potentially available on a typical primary residence in this neighborhood. That is a genuine difference in cost and timeline compared with a full probate, and whether a specific property qualifies is a question for the probate attorney.

What I do not do:

I do not give legal advice, interpret the will or the trust, advise on whether probate is required, or opine on a personal representative's or trustee's duties. That belongs with the probate attorney.

I do not advise on the estate tax, the basis step-up under Internal Revenue Code section 1014, or the income tax consequences of a sale by the estate. That belongs with the CPA.

I do not take instructions from one beneficiary over the others.

What I do:

I provide the date-of-death valuation support and the current market valuation the attorney and the CPA both need, with comparable sales documented.

I flag the real estate issues that change the timeline, including the title vesting, any lien or recording problem, the condition and vacancy questions, and the insurance exposure on a vacant property. Estates miss that one most often.

I carry the property through a sale that meets the court's confirmation requirements where those apply, and I keep the personal representative out of the day-to-day coordination.

I give the family the holding-cost number, so that the choice between selling now and holding through a longer process is a decision made against a real monthly figure instead of a feeling.

The expensive pattern is always the same. An estate holds a vacant property for months while the family decides, insurance on a vacant home is quietly more expensive or quietly lapsed, the carry runs every month, and the eventual sale price is no better than it would have been at the start.

What are property taxes in Oakland?

I use 1.71 percent in this report, the Oakland effective rate. On a home at $710,647 that is roughly $1,013 a month, $12,152 a year. What is the transfer tax in Oakland?

This is the cost people forget to budget for, and it varies more by city line than anything else in a California transaction. Oakland is a chartered city, so the rate here is $16.10 per $1,000 of value. On a home at $710,647 that is $11,441. For scale, the identical sale price would carry $782 in Dublin and $11,441 in Oakland, both in this same county. There is also a cliff worth knowing about. The rate applies to the whole value, not just the portion above a threshold, so crossing $2,000,000 reprices the entire sale. At exactly $2,000,000 the tax is $32,200. What first-time buyer help is available in Oakland?

CalHFA runs the statewide programs and they layer on top of a first mortgage and do not replace it. MyHome is a deferred-payment junior loan toward the down payment and closing costs, open year round. ZIP covers closing costs on a CalHFA conventional first. Dream For All is the shared-appreciation program, limited to first-generation buyers, and it runs in announced windows, not continuously. GSFA Platinum is a separate option, and Oakland and Alameda County run their own programs on their own funding cycles. Those are the ones most people never hear about. I would establish the price a program allows first, then shop inside it. Doing it the other way around is how people fall for a house the assistance cannot reach.

Can I build an ADU in Oakland?

Almost certainly yes, in some form. State law requires local agencies to allow accessory dwelling units on residential lots with ministerial approval and a decision deadline, so the real question is what size and configuration the local ordinance permits, not whether you are allowed. Owner-occupancy requirements on new ADUs have been removed, so the main house and the ADU can both be rented. Selling one separately is a different question. Assembly Bill 1033 lets a city allow an ADU to be sold as its own condominium unit, but it is permissive and not mandatory, relatively few cities have adopted an enabling ordinance, and without one there is no right to do it. That is a question for the Oakland planning department and the municipal code, not something the state law answers on its own.

What Fremont actually is

Fremont is five towns that agreed to become one city on January 23, 1956, and it still reads that way on the ground.

Mission San Jose, Centerville, Niles, Irvington and Warm Springs each had their own main street, their own cemetery and their own reason for existing before the merger. Wally Pond, a former councilmember, is the one who got it through. Washington Township had organized the area back in 1853. Newark was asked to join, its voters declined, and it incorporated separately in 1955. The city took its name from John C. Fremont, who mapped a route through Mission Pass into the rest of the bay.

The result is a city that does not have one center. It has five, and the distance between them is why a buyer who has only looked at Fremont on a map tends to be surprised in person. Old Mission San Jose, at 43300 Mission Boulevard, was founded on June 11, 1797 and keeps the oldest standing adobe building in Alameda County, put up in 1809. Niles has a pre war commercial street and the studio ground where Charlie Chaplin shot five films in 1915. Warm Springs has what is left of the winery Leland Stanford bought in 1869 and his brother Josiah built out to 350 acres. Centerville has a cemetery from 1855 with the road names buried in it. Ardenwood has a redwood farmhouse from 1857 with an 1889 Queen Anne addition by Samuel Newsom.

The modern economy sits at the south end. General Motors opened Fremont Assembly in 1963, closed it in 1982, reopened it with Toyota as NUMMI in 1984, and that venture built more than eight million vehicles before Toyota shut it in April 2010 and 4,700 people lost their jobs. Tesla reopened it that October and built close to 560,000 vehicles there in 2023.

So treat Fremont as five housing markets instead of one. The year built swings from mission era ground to current construction, sometimes within a mile, and the inspection list, the tax bill and the commute are all different in each of the five. Pick the district first and the house second. And drive your actual commute, at your actual hour, before you decide which of the five you can live in.

Historic districts

Centerville

Typical home value $930,526, down 0.14 percent month over month, down 2.9 percent year over year. Full Centerville report.

There is a cemetery at Post Street and Bonde Way that is older than the city around it by a century.

Centerville Pioneer Cemetery goes back to 1855, when the Alameda Presbyterian Church, organized two years earlier, bought the ground under the Reverend William Wallace Brier and nine other founding members. California recognized it as a state cemetery in 1859 and it went into the California Register of Historic Resources in 1976. The names in it are the names on the road signs. Herman Eggers is buried there. So is Blacow, and Blacow Road still runs through this part of Fremont.

That is the useful thing to understand about Centerville. It was one of five separate townships, with Mission San Jose, Niles, Irvington and Warm Springs, and all five voted to become one city on January 23, 1956. Washington Township had organized the area as far back as 1853. Newark was invited into the merger and its voters said no. Newark is a separate city today and incorporated on its own in 1955. Centerville was a town with a main street before Fremont existed, and the commercial spine along Fremont Boulevard and Peralta Boulevard is the remains of it.

What that means on the ground. The housing here is older and denser than the tract neighborhoods east of it, with a mix that includes genuine pre war stock, post war infill and apartment buildings along the arterials. It is also the part of Fremont closest to the Centerville train station. For anyone commuting south that is a real factor.

If you are looking here, do two things. Pull the year built on every address you are serious about, because the range on a single street can be fifty years and the inspection list is completely different at either end. Then go stand at the Centerville station during the evening commute. Rail access is most of why people pay to be on this side of Fremont, and whether the schedule actually fits your day is something you can settle in one trip instead of after you move.

Irvington

Typical home value $1,244,258, down 0.08 percent month over month, down 3.2 percent year over year. Full Irvington report.

In 1884 a man named Juan Gallegos built a winery here that was designed to work by gravity. Grapes went in at the high end and wine came out the bottom. By 1893 the Palmdale Company was farming 600 acres of vineyard, crushing 2,400 tons of grapes a year, and holding over 1,250,000 gallons in barrel. It had one of the two largest wine cellars in California.

On April 18, 1906 the earthquake that wrecked San Francisco wrecked it too. The winery was demolished afterward and the oak cooperage was sold off to the California Wine Association. The ruins are still standing at Washington Boulevard and Osgood Road. The Sisters of the Holy Family bought the estate in 1948. If BART ever builds the optional Irvington station, it will sit next to a century old winery ruin.

The center of Irvington is Five Corners, where Fremont Boulevard bends and Bay Street, Washington Boulevard and Union Street all come in. It has been called Washington Corners and simply The Corners, and today it is also called Irvington Square. Clark's Hall stands there, the oldest documented commercial building in Fremont. So does the Irvington Monument, put up in 1917 by the Irvington Chamber of Commerce to work as a roundabout. By the 1960s streetlights had made it pointless and drivers kept hitting it, so the city pulled it out. It was returned to its present spot in 1979.

Irvington was one of the five townships that voted to become Fremont on January 23, 1956, and it still has a real main street. Most of Fremont does not.

So the housing here is older than the city average and more varied. Across Fremont only about 2 percent of the housing stock predates 1950, and the pockets that do are in places like this.

Pull the year built on any address before anything else, because the range on one street can be sixty years. On the older stock, ask about foundation bolting, wiring and the sewer lateral. And walk Five Corners once. The bend in the road is the reason the neighborhood is shaped the way it is.

Mission San Jose

Typical home value $2,140,736, up 0.36 percent month over month, down 0.0 percent year over year. Full Mission San Jose report.

The oldest standing adobe building in Alameda County is in this neighborhood, and it has been there since 1809.

Old Mission San Jose sits at 43300 Mission Boulevard, at the Washington Boulevard intersection. The mission was founded on June 11, 1797, the fourteenth in Alta California. The 1868 earthquake took down the adobe church and most of what stood around it. The building that survived is the one from 1809, originally the padres living quarters and now the museum. The church you see is a reconstruction, begun in 1981 and finished in 1985, built with period materials and period methods, not a modern frame behind a facade.

Go into the garden. The olive trees in it are roughly two hundred years old and date to the mission period, and the mission orchards once supplied oil and fruit well beyond this valley. A town grew up around the mission with its own post office from 1850, and grapes, nursery plants and olives carried the local economy for decades before anyone subdivided anything.

The land reads the same way today. This is the eastern edge of Fremont where the valley floor runs up into the hills, so the lots on the uphill side sit higher and look back across the bay, and the streets bend to follow the grade instead of running on a grid.

What you are buying here. A mix that runs from mission era ground through mid century tracts to much newer infill. Two houses four blocks apart can be forty years apart in construction type. Read the year built before you read anything else. On the older stock, ask about foundation bolting and about the sewer lateral. On the hillside parcels, ask for the soils and drainage history, because grade is what moves cost on a hill lot. Then drive Mission Boulevard north to Washington and back at five in the evening on a weekday, not a Sunday. The commute is the thing people get wrong about this neighborhood, and ten minutes in actual traffic settles it.

Niles

Typical home value $1,407,313, up 0.09 percent month over month, down 2.3 percent year over year. Full Niles report.

Charlie Chaplin made five pictures on one block of Niles Boulevard, and you can still sit in a building there and watch them.

Gilbert Anderson, who the country knew as Broncho Billy, went looking for somewhere with mild winters and country that read as the West on film. He picked Niles. Construction on the full Essanay studio began in 1913 to a design by George W. Page, two hundred feet long and fifty wide, with a glass enclosed stage to catch the daylight, Western street sets, and a horse stable behind. Chaplin shot his first Essanay picture in Chicago and came out here for the rest. The Tramp, 1915, was his sixth film for the company and the fifth he made in Niles. A Night Out came out that February and The Champion in March. He left for Mutual in 1916 and the studio was finished by 1918.

The Niles Essanay Silent Film Museum is at 37417 Niles Boulevard, in the Edison Theater, a century old nickelodeon a short walk from where the studio stood. They run silent programs with live accompaniment. Go once, on a weekend, and the street stops reading as a quiet commercial strip.

The older thing in Niles is the canyon. Niles Canyon carried the final stretch of the First Transcontinental Railroad, and the eleven mile section between Sunol and Niles went on the National Register in October 2010 as the Niles Canyon Transcontinental Railroad Historic District. The Pacific Locomotive Association leased the right of way and started rebuilding track in 1987. The first passenger train ran from Sunol in May 1988, and it still runs.

What that means for a house here. Niles is the one part of Fremont with a genuine pre war main street, and the housing around it is older and smaller than the tract stock that covers most of the city. Expect pre war construction and the inspection items that come with it. You are also at the mouth of a canyon, so ask specifically about drainage and about the creek during a wet winter before you write an offer. Walk Niles Boulevard end to end on a Saturday. Ten minutes on foot tells you more about whether this is your neighborhood than any listing photograph will.

Warm Springs

Typical home value $1,711,399, up 0.39 percent month over month, up 1.1 percent year over year. Full Warm Springs report.

Leland Stanford bought a resort here in 1869 and his brother turned it into a winery that held half a million gallons.

Josiah Stanford ran the operation and built the vineyard out to 350 acres, with winery capacity around 500,000 gallons. The site is California Historical Landmark 642. Most of that ground is housing now and a single brick building is what is left. That is the usual ending for Bay Area agriculture, though not usually this close to a BART platform. The Weibel name on the neighborhood immediately north is the next chapter of the same story, a champagne operation that worked the Stanford site after the Stanfords.

The other thing about Warm Springs is the plant at the south end of Fremont. General Motors broke ground on Fremont Assembly in September 1961 and the first car came off the line in May 1963. GM closed it in 1982. It reopened in 1984 as NUMMI, a joint venture between GM and Toyota that rehired about 85 percent of the laid off workforce and then produced more than eight million vehicles, peaking at 428,633 in 2006. Toyota shut it in April 2010 and roughly 4,700 people lost their jobs. Tesla bought it at a steep discount and reopened it that October. In 2023 it built close to 560,000 vehicles and employed more than twenty thousand people.

That matters here in a way it does not matter in most neighborhoods. Two large employers inside two decades, with a hard stop in between, is the local economic history, and the housing demand in south Fremont tracks it.

What to do with that if you are buying. Warm Springs has the newest housing stock in this part of the city and a BART station, so you are competing with buyers who are pricing the commute more than the house. Check the year built, check whether the unit is in an HOA and what the dues cover, and get the assessment history, because a lot of the newer construction here sits inside districts that carry additional levies on top of the base tax rate. Pull the tax bill on any specific address before you decide what you can afford. The difference between two otherwise comparable homes here is often on that bill and not in the listing.

Other areas

Ardenwood

Typical home value $1,385,254, down 0.07 percent month over month, down 2.4 percent year over year. Full Ardenwood report.

A Pennsylvania farmer named George Washington Patterson came to California for gold in 1849, found almost none, and in 1857 built a redwood farmhouse here instead. His neighbor James Hawley helped him put it up.

That house is still standing, and it is the reason this neighborhood has a name that sounds like an estate. Patterson had reached Alameda County by 1851 and kept buying land. In 1889 the family brought in Samuel Newsom, one of the better known Victorian architects working in California, and wrapped the plain farmhouse in a Queen Anne addition. Three generations of Pattersons lived in it. In 1984 the family gave 131 acres at the center of the property to the City of Fremont, and since 1985 the East Bay Regional Park District has run it as a working farm kept at its turn of the century condition.

Ardenwood Historic Farm is the result. Draft horses, crops, a narrow gauge train, and docents in period clothing running tours of the Patterson House on Thursdays, Fridays and Sundays during the season. It is the best hour you can spend with a kid in this city, and most people in Fremont have never gone.

The neighborhood around it is a different era entirely. This is Fremont's northwest corner, laid out late and built out as planned subdivision, so the housing stock is newer and more uniform than anywhere in the historic districts, with a lot of it inside homeowner associations. The Dumbarton Bridge approach is right here. That is the whole reason a certain kind of buyer looks at Ardenwood first. this is the Fremont neighborhood with the shortest drive to the Peninsula.

So buy it for that, but verify it. Drive the bridge at eight in the morning on a Tuesday before you commit, because the approach backs up and the map time is not the real time. Then get the HOA documents and the reserve study, not just the dues figure. On newer planned development the dues are easy to find and the reserve position is what actually costs you later.

Baylands

This is the edge of Fremont where the city stops and the bay starts, and most of it is federally protected.

The Don Edwards San Francisco Bay National Wildlife Refuge was established in 1972 after years of local campaigning against the filling of the bayfront. Arthur Ogilvie helped organize the committee that pushed for it. Congress named the refuge for Congressman Don Edwards in 1995, and the Fremont visitor center opened in 1979. Mowry Slough, in Fremont, is inside the refuge. One source gives the founding year as 1974, but the Fish and Wildlife Service and the Federal Register both say 1972.

That history is the whole character of the Baylands. The reason there is open tidal marsh at the end of Fremont instead of more subdivision is that people fought to keep it, and then the federal government took it on.

So there is very little housing out here, and that is the first thing to understand if you are searching the Baylands. What people usually mean is the newer development inland of the marsh instead of anything on it. Fremont has 81,579 housing units in total and about 2 percent predate 1950, so what exists on this side is recent, planned, and often inside an association.

Three things to establish on a specific address. Get the flood zone determination with a written insurance quote before your contingencies expire, because this is low flat ground at the bay margin and the premium is a real monthly number, not a closing detail. Ask the inspector about settlement and look hard at foundations and flatwork, since ground near filled marsh behaves differently from ground on the valley floor. And on anything in a homeowners association, read the reserve study, not just the dues.

Then go to the refuge visitor center and walk out on the levee trail. It is the one part of Fremont where you can stand in the middle of a landscape that looks the way the whole bay margin used to look, and it is a short drive from houses that cost well over a million dollars.

Brookvale

Typical home value $1,767,875, down 0.12 percent month over month, up 0.5 percent year over year. Full Brookvale report.

Brookvale is one of the Fremont neighborhoods where the published history is genuinely thin, so this is the development record and the practical read, not a story somebody made up.

What the sources say, and they disagree, is that Brookvale filled in later than the 1950s and 1960s tracts, with one guide putting much of the housing in the 1970s and 1980s as demand spread south from the bay and north from Silicon Valley. Treat the decade as approximate until somebody checks the tract maps at the county. For context, the five townships of Centerville, Irvington, Mission San Jose, Niles and Warm Springs voted to become one city on January 23, 1956, so anything built here in the 1970s went up into a city that was already twenty years old and filling in its last open ground.

The sourced figures are the city's and they are solid. Fremont has 81,579 housing units. 977 were built in 1939 or earlier and 749 between 1940 and 1949, so about 2 percent of the city predates 1950. 45,927 units, 56.3 percent, are single family detached. Owner occupancy is 60.8 percent citywide. The ACS median value for an owner occupied Fremont home is 1,403,800 dollars, median gross rent is 2,933, and the median listing on the market is about 1,489 square feet.

If the later build era holds for Brookvale, that has real consequences and they are mostly in your favour. Houses from the 1970s and 1980s generally have copper instead of galvanized supply lines, modern panel capacity, and foundations built to later code, so the pre war inspection list that dominates Oakland and Berkeley does not apply here.

What does apply: ask about the roof and its age, the water heater, the furnace and the condition of any original dual pane windows, because those are the systems that reach end of life on a house of this vintage. Ask whether the house sits in a homeowners association, since a lot of Fremont development from that era does, and if it does, read the reserve study, not just the dues figure.

Then check the lot dimensions and the setbacks if an ADU is part of your plan, and drive your commute at the hour you would actually drive it. In a neighborhood without a landmark to anchor it, the commute and the house itself are the whole decision.

Cabrillo

Typical home value $1,332,071, down 0.05 percent month over month, up 0.1 percent year over year. Full Cabrillo report.

Cabrillo is a 1960s tract neighborhood in north Fremont, and the useful facts about it are in the development record instead of in any story.

Cabrillo Elementary sits at 36700 San Pedro Drive, and the street names around it run Spanish. That is where the neighborhood's name comes from. Local guides put the typical house here at three bedrooms, two baths and somewhere between 1,100 and 1,500 square feet, built in the 1960s, mostly traditional ranch. Those figures come from real estate sources instead of from the assessor, so treat them as the shape of the stock and not as gospel.

The sourced picture for the city around it is firmer. Fremont has 81,579 housing units. 977 of them were built in 1939 or earlier and 749 between 1940 and 1949, so about 2 percent of the city predates 1950. 45,927 units, or 56.3 percent, are single family detached. Owner occupancy citywide is 60.8 percent. The ACS median value for an owner occupied home in Fremont is 1,403,800 dollars and the median gross rent is 2,933. The median listing on the market runs about 1,489 square feet, putting the typical Cabrillo house at or a little under the citywide middle.

That matters more than it sounds. A neighborhood of near identical houses from one decade is the easiest kind of market to price and the hardest kind to overpay in, provided you do the comparison properly.

So pull the year built and the square footage and compare only against the same era and size. Then look at what has been done to the house. In a tract where the bones are all the same, the entire price spread is condition and updates, and that is a negotiation you can win with an inspection report.

Check the lot dimensions and the setbacks if an ADU is part of the plan. Single story on a regular lot is the best case in this city.

Then walk the block in the evening. In a neighborhood this uniform, the street is the variable.

City Center

On September 6, 1960 the Fremont City Council picked the Fudenna ranch as the site for its civic center. The city was four years old and it was choosing where its middle would be.

What got built there is the reason this neighborhood exists. Central Park started development in 1960. The Hub shopping center opened in October 1962. The city government building went up in 1968. Lake Elizabeth was created in 1968 by excavating part of Stivers Lagoon Marsh, and the city dedicated it on March 22, 1969 to its sister city, Elizabeth in Australia. The lake runs 82 acres inside 174 acres that Alameda County Flood Control leases to the city on condition it stays park and recreation land. Central Park is around 450 acres now and the paved loop around the lake is about two miles.

One detail worth knowing. Stivers Lagoon is one of a chain of freshwater marshes strung along the east side of the Hayward Fault. The lake is where it is because the fault put water there.

So City Center is a planned civic core: government buildings, a regional shopping center, and a very large park, with housing built around them from the 1960s forward. Fremont as a whole has 81,579 housing units and only about 2 percent of them predate 1950, so essentially everything here is postwar or newer.

What that means for a purchase. This is the densest part of Fremont and a lot of the inventory is attached housing and condominiums. On anything in an association, get the budget and the reserve study, not just the dues figure, because the reserve position is what turns into a special assessment. Ask what the association insures and what you have to.

Then check what is entitled nearby. City Center is where Fremont has concentrated new development and a view or an open parcel is not a protected asset. The city's plans are public documents and worth twenty minutes before you commit.

And walk the lake loop once. Two miles of flat paved path at your doorstep is the actual amenity here.

Glenmoor

Typical home value $1,647,223, up 0.44 percent month over month, down 0.1 percent year over year. Full Glenmoor report.

Glenmoor has a homeowners association that was incorporated in March 1953, when there were no more than 75 houses standing in the whole subdivision. It was probably the first one in the Fremont area, and it predates the city of Fremont by three years.

The developer was Glenmoor Homes, Inc., formed in 1951 by James R. Meyer with the civil engineer Ralph E. Cotter Jr., the contractors James L. and Robert H. Reeder, and a second civil engineer, Fred T. Duvall. They filed the first tract map in November 1951 and kept going until 1966, laying out 32 separate tracts over fifteen years and buying the land for the next ones while they built the current ones. By March 1958 there were more than 700 houses in above Blacow. The finished neighborhood runs to roughly 1,900 single family homes.

Before that it was farms. Seven large ones covered this ground, and the family names on them are the names you now drive on and look up in the Centerville cemetery: Garrett Norris, Herman Eggers, Robert Blacow, Martin Brophy, Ashley Cameron.

What it is now is the clearest example of a postwar ranch neighborhood in central Fremont. Single story, generous lots by Bay Area standards, wide streets, and two parks the association still runs, Meyer Park and Alta Park.

That consistency is the thing to use. Because 1,900 houses went up from one developer inside fifteen years, the comparable sales here are genuinely comparable. That is rare, and it makes pricing unusually readable. Original ranch houses on large lots are also the best ADU candidates in Fremont, so if that is in your plan, check the lot dimensions and the setbacks before you fall for a floor plan.

Two things to confirm on any specific address. Find out which of the 32 tracts it sits in and what year it was built, because the early 1950s houses and the mid 1960s houses are not the same product. And read the association documents on what is enforced, since a neighborhood that organized itself in 1953 generally has opinions. Then walk it in the evening. The street life is the amenity here.

Grimmer

Typical home value $1,375,798, up 0.33 percent month over month, up 0.5 percent year over year. Full Grimmer report.

Grimmer is named for a family nobody has documented properly, and the honest thing is to say so.

E. M. Grimmer gave the name to Grimmer Boulevard and to E. M. Grimmer Elementary at 43030 Newport Drive, and the published record on who that was is thin. Somebody with a morning at the Fremont Museum of Local History could probably settle it. Until then, treat the name as a pioneer family name of the Washington Township era and leave it there, because inventing a biography is worse than admitting a gap.

What is documented is the development record, and that is the useful part anyway. This is south Fremont, east of the old Irvington township, built out as tract housing in the postwar decades. Across the city there are 81,579 housing units and only about 2 percent of them were built before 1950. Fremont is a postwar city and this is a postwar neighborhood. Citywide, 56.3 percent of units are single family detached, owner occupancy runs 60.8 percent, and the median listing is around 1,489 square feet.

So here is how to shop it. Single builder tracts from a narrow window make for honest comparables, so pull the year built and the tract on any address and then compare only within that era. Two houses four streets apart can be different products from different decades.

Get the lot dimensions and the setbacks early if an accessory dwelling unit matters to you. Single story houses on regular lots are Fremont's best ADU candidates, and what stops people is almost always the setback, not the floor plan.

Run the ordinary inspection list for the era: the roof, the original galvanized or copper supply lines, the panel capacity if you are planning to add electric load, and the condition of any aluminum wiring if the house is from the right years for it.

Then drive to the nearest freeway on ramp at your actual commute hour. South Fremont lives on that answer.

Kimber-Gomes

Typical home value $2,381,985, up 0.90 percent month over month, up 2.5 percent year over year. Full Kimber-Gomes report.

This neighborhood is named after a chicken farm and a school board member, and the chicken farm was world famous.

John E. Kimber started Kimber Farms at Niles in 1925. The detailed company history puts the starting flock at 800 hens, though a local museum exhibit says 80,000, so take the smaller figure as the better documented one. Kimber was born in New York in 1895 and came out of Stanford in 1917 with a major in agriculture and a minor in music. The farm lost money for seven straight years and turned its first profit in 1932. Then it did something almost nobody in the industry was doing: it hired a full time geneticist, becoming only the second poultry breeder anywhere to employ one. Accounts differ on the year, with the company history saying 1934 and another source naming Dr W. F. Lamoreux in 1943. It also put a veterinarian on part time to autopsy every bird that died so the cause could be traced through each family line.

Kimber Farms incorporated on December 3, 1946, with Niles as headquarters and operations at Atascadero, Los Gatos, Sanger and Clovis. Its 1955 catalogue was about building a more profitable White Leghorn. By the 1940s through the 1960s the operation had international standing, and the USDA's National Agricultural Library holds its records today.

The Gomes half honors John Gomes, from a pioneer farming family that worked the fields nearby. He sat on the Irvington School Board for years and served as a director of the Alameda County Water District. John Gomes Elementary was built for the Way Out West and Mission Valley housing, took its first students in September 1969 and was dedicated in March 1970. The building is a six sided pod design and it still looks like nothing else around it.

So the housing here is late 1960s and after, built on former farm and ranch ground. That is the Fremont pattern. Only about 2 percent of the city's stock predates 1950.

Pull the year built and the lot dimensions. On single story houses of this era with generous lots, check setbacks early if an ADU is in your plan. Then go look at the Gomes pod building. You will not mistake it for anything else.

Mission Valley

Typical home value $2,434,564, up 0.74 percent month over month, up 2.9 percent year over year. Full Mission Valley report.

Mission Valley is the ground between the old mission and the valley floor, and it was built as housing tracts in the late 1960s.

The clearest documentary trace of that is a school. John Gomes Elementary, a short way off, was built specifically to serve what the district at the time called the Way Out West and Mission Valley housing, took its first students in September 1969 and was dedicated in March 1970. Mission Valley Elementary sits in the neighborhood at 41700 Denise Street. When a school district builds schools for a named housing project, that is the development record telling you when the neighborhood arrived.

The anchor to the north is Old Mission San Jose at 43300 Mission Boulevard, founded on June 11, 1797 and holding the oldest standing adobe building in Alameda County, put up in 1809. This neighborhood is named for its position relative to that, not for any mission era settlement of its own.

The housing is accordingly postwar tract: single family, laid out to a plan, on a street pattern that follows the rising grade toward the hills. Across Fremont, 81,579 housing units and only about 2 percent of them predate 1950. The city median listing runs around 1,489 square feet and the ACS median owner occupied value for Fremont is 1,403,800 dollars, with owner occupancy at 60.8 percent citywide.

The two price numbers that disagree, and why

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Fremont is down 1.5 percent year over year. Property tax, and the three rules that decide your bill

On a home at $1,439,805 in Fremont, property tax runs about $1,704 a month, or $20,445 a year, at the effective rate used throughout this report. Three rules decide what any given owner here actually pays, and they produce very different bills on identical houses.

Proposition 13 sets the base at 1 percent of assessed value and caps the growth of that assessed value at 2 percent per year while one owner holds the property. Voter-approved bonds and special assessments sit on top of the 1 percent base. The real rate runs above 1 percent almost everywhere in California. The effective rate used in this article is 1.42 percent, the Alameda County effective rate, sourced to Ownwell property tax trends.

The rule that surprises buyers is reassessment. A long-time owner next door can pay a fraction of what a new buyer pays on an identical house, because their assessed value has been climbing at 2 percent a year from a purchase decades ago while a new purchase resets assessed value to the price paid. Any property tax figure you read that was calculated from median taxes actually paid will understate a new purchase substantially, because it is averaging in those protected assessments. This article uses the new-buyer basis instead.

The second rule is the supplemental bill. A purchase triggers a reassessment, and the difference between the seller's old assessed value and the new one arrives as a separate supplemental tax bill, often months after closing and often after the escrow impound account was already set up on the old number. It is a real and routine shortfall in the first year of ownership.

The third rule is Proposition 19. Since 2021 it has governed two things that matter here. A homeowner who is 55 or older, or severely disabled, or a wildfire or disaster victim, can transfer their existing assessed value to a replacement home anywhere in California, up to three times, with an upward adjustment if the replacement costs more. That is the rule that makes a move financially possible for long-time owners who would otherwise be locked in place by their own tax basis. Proposition 19 also narrowed the parent to child exclusion sharply. An inherited home now keeps the parent's assessed value only if a child makes it their own primary residence and files on time, and even then only up to a value cap, with the excess reassessed. Inherited property that becomes a rental or a second home gets reassessed to market value. Families who assumed the old rules still applied are the ones who get a tax bill they did not plan for.

ADUs, and what they are actually worth here

At $1,439,805 typical value and $844 per square foot in Fremont, added permitted square footage is the most direct equity lever available to an owner here, and an accessory dwelling unit is the version of that with its own rules.

California law now requires local agencies to allow accessory dwelling units on residential lots, with ministerial approval and a decision deadline, and it has steadily removed the obstacles cities used to rely on. Owner-occupancy requirements on new ADUs have been eliminated. Both the main house and the ADU can be rented. Impact fees are waived for smaller units. Size limits are calculated on interior livable space.

The provision with the largest equity consequence is Assembly Bill 1033. It allows a city to let an ADU be sold separately from the main house as its own condominium unit. The important detail, and the one that most coverage gets wrong, is that AB 1033 is permissive and not mandatory. The state allows cities to opt in. It does not require them to. A relatively small number of California cities have adopted an enabling ordinance so far, and in a city that has not adopted one, the statute gives an owner no right to sell an ADU separately. Whether it applies to a given property is a question for that city's planning department and its municipal code, not a question that can be answered from the state law alone. Where it has been adopted, the process is demanding: a condominium plan, compliance with subdivision law, a safety inspection before recording, and consent from every existing lienholder. That last step stops most attempts.

The practical valuation point for an owner here is simpler. An ADU adds value in two ways that behave very differently. Rental income supports value through the income it produces, and appraisers in this market treat that unevenly depending on whether the unit is permitted and legally rentable. Permitted square footage adds value more directly and more reliably. Unpermitted square footage frequently adds the least value of all, because it introduces a disclosure obligation, a lender problem, and a negotiating lever for the buyer. The gap between a permitted conversion and an identical unpermitted one is the single largest avoidable value difference in this category.

What I see as the listing agent, repeatedly:

Selling a home in probate or from a trust

The most expensive thing an estate can do with a house in Fremont is nothing, for several months, while the family decides. At $13,361 a month in carry, that is $80,164 over half a year, and the statutory fee structure does not shrink while they wait.

The structural fact that drives everything in California is that statutory probate fees are calculated on the gross value of the estate's assets, not on the family's equity. California Probate Code section 10810 sets the attorney fee schedule, and section 10800 applies the same schedule to the personal representative. The schedule runs 4 percent on the first 100,000 dollars of gross value, 3 percent on the next 100,000, 2 percent on the next 800,000, and 1 percent on the next 9 million. The mortgage does not reduce the calculation. A home worth a great deal with very little equity still generates fees on the full value.

Run it on this neighborhood. A home at the typical $1,439,805 generates a statutory attorney fee of about $27,398 under that schedule, and the personal representative is entitled to the same amount under section 10800, for a combined statutory cost of roughly $54,796 before court filing fees, probate referee fees, publication costs or any of the secondary expenses. If the home carries a large mortgage, that figure does not move. It is calculated on the $1,439,805, not on what is left after the bank.

Assembly Bill 2016 changed the picture for smaller estates. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, the revised Probate Code section 13150 allows a streamlined petition to transfer a decedent's primary residence valued up to 750,000 dollars without a full probate. The statutory fee schedule does not apply. The threshold applies to the primary residence only, and non-residential real property remains subject to a far lower threshold under section 13200. For Fremont specifically, this is the part that does not help. The typical home here is $1,439,805. That is $689,805 above the 750,000 dollar threshold, so the streamlined petition does not reach the typical property in this neighborhood. It does reach homes in the lower-value neighborhoods in the ranking table above, and it reaches a meaningful share of the housing stock in the inland parts of this region. That is worth knowing for a family with property in more than one area.

What I do not do:

I do not give legal advice, interpret the will or the trust, advise on whether probate is required, or opine on a personal representative's or trustee's duties. That belongs with the probate attorney.

I do not advise on the estate tax, the basis step-up under Internal Revenue Code section 1014, or the income tax consequences of a sale by the estate. That belongs with the CPA.

I do not take instructions from one beneficiary over the others.

What I do:

I provide the date-of-death valuation support and the current market valuation the attorney and the CPA both need, with comparable sales documented.

I flag the real estate issues that change the timeline, including the title vesting, any lien or recording problem, the condition and vacancy questions, and the insurance exposure on a vacant property. Estates miss that one most often.

I carry the property through a sale that meets the court's confirmation requirements where those apply, and I keep the personal representative out of the day-to-day coordination.

I give the family the holding-cost number, so that the choice between selling now and holding through a longer process is a decision made against a real monthly figure instead of a feeling.

The expensive pattern is always the same. An estate holds a vacant property for months while the family decides, insurance on a vacant home is quietly more expensive or quietly lapsed, the carry runs every month, and the eventual sale price is no better than it would have been at the start.

Can I build an ADU in Fremont?

What Hayward actually is

Hayward called itself the Apricot City, and it meant it.

The town incorporated on March 11, 1876 under the name Haywards, with an s, taken from the hotel the settlement had grown around. What made it was fruit. From the second half of the nineteenth century this was one of California's leading fruit growing districts, sending cherries, apricots, peaches, tomatoes and potatoes out by rail. Hunt Brothers opened a cannery here in 1895 and ran it as a seasonal industry large enough to build worker cottages and community buildings around itself. By 1950 the city had about 14,000 residents.

Two other stories sit in that landscape. In 1918 Zenjuro Shibata moved out from Oakland and founded Mt. Eden Nursery on a marshy plot, recorded in his eldest son Yoshio's name because California law then barred him from holding it in his own. The first greenhouse went up that year in carnations and the family switched to roses in the mid 1930s. The nursery ran into the 1980s and the Shibata Garden at 25941 Industrial Boulevard is what remains of it. And out on the bay flats there was a separate community called Russell City, named in the 1850s or laid out in 1907 depending on which account you take. The county removed it by eminent domain by 1964 and put an industrial park on the ground.

After the Second World War the orchards went under tracts, and that is the city you see now. Hayward has 52,243 housing units and only 9.1 percent of them predate 1950, so this is overwhelmingly a postwar place. 51.2 percent are single family detached, owner occupancy is 58.0 percent, the ACS median value is 854,400 dollars and the median gross rent is 2,391.

One thing applies everywhere here and nowhere else so strongly. The Hayward Fault runs the length of this city. Ask the title company whether any parcel you are serious about sits inside an Alquist-Priolo earthquake fault zone, and ask whether the house has ever been bolted to its foundation. In Hayward that is the first question, not the last.

The two price numbers that disagree, and why

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Hayward is down 2.4 percent year over year. They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. Property tax, and the three rules that decide your bill

On a home at $816,424 in Hayward, property tax runs about $966 a month, or $11,593 a year, at the effective rate used throughout this report. Three rules decide what any given owner here actually pays, and they produce very different bills on identical houses.

Proposition 13 sets the base at 1 percent of assessed value and caps the growth of that assessed value at 2 percent per year while one owner holds the property. Voter-approved bonds and special assessments sit on top of the 1 percent base. The real rate runs above 1 percent almost everywhere in California. The effective rate used in this article is 1.42 percent, the Alameda County effective rate, sourced to Ownwell property tax trends.

The rule that surprises buyers is reassessment. A long-time owner next door can pay a fraction of what a new buyer pays on an identical house, because their assessed value has been climbing at 2 percent a year from a purchase decades ago while a new purchase resets assessed value to the price paid. Any property tax figure you read that was calculated from median taxes actually paid will understate a new purchase substantially, because it is averaging in those protected assessments. This article uses the new-buyer basis instead.

The second rule is the supplemental bill. A purchase triggers a reassessment, and the difference between the seller's old assessed value and the new one arrives as a separate supplemental tax bill, often months after closing and often after the escrow impound account was already set up on the old number. It is a real and routine shortfall in the first year of ownership.

The third rule is Proposition 19. Since 2021 it has governed two things that matter here. A homeowner who is 55 or older, or severely disabled, or a wildfire or disaster victim, can transfer their existing assessed value to a replacement home anywhere in California, up to three times, with an upward adjustment if the replacement costs more. That is the rule that makes a move financially possible for long-time owners who would otherwise be locked in place by their own tax basis. Proposition 19 also narrowed the parent to child exclusion sharply. An inherited home now keeps the parent's assessed value only if a child makes it their own primary residence and files on time, and even then only up to a value cap, with the excess reassessed. Inherited property that becomes a rental or a second home gets reassessed to market value. Families who assumed the old rules still applied are the ones who get a tax bill they did not plan for.

ADUs, and what they are actually worth here

At $816,424 typical value and $553 per square foot in Hayward, added permitted square footage is the most direct equity lever available to an owner here, and an accessory dwelling unit is the version of that with its own rules.

California law now requires local agencies to allow accessory dwelling units on residential lots, with ministerial approval and a decision deadline, and it has steadily removed the obstacles cities used to rely on. Owner-occupancy requirements on new ADUs have been eliminated. Both the main house and the ADU can be rented. Impact fees are waived for smaller units. Size limits are calculated on interior livable space.

The provision with the largest equity consequence is Assembly Bill 1033. It allows a city to let an ADU be sold separately from the main house as its own condominium unit. The important detail, and the one that most coverage gets wrong, is that AB 1033 is permissive and not mandatory. The state allows cities to opt in. It does not require them to. A relatively small number of California cities have adopted an enabling ordinance so far, and in a city that has not adopted one, the statute gives an owner no right to sell an ADU separately. Whether it applies to a given property is a question for that city's planning department and its municipal code, not a question that can be answered from the state law alone. Where it has been adopted, the process is demanding: a condominium plan, compliance with subdivision law, a safety inspection before recording, and consent from every existing lienholder. That last step stops most attempts.

The practical valuation point for an owner here is simpler. An ADU adds value in two ways that behave very differently. Rental income supports value through the income it produces, and appraisers in this market treat that unevenly depending on whether the unit is permitted and legally rentable. Permitted square footage adds value more directly and more reliably. Unpermitted square footage frequently adds the least value of all, because it introduces a disclosure obligation, a lender problem, and a negotiating lever for the buyer. The gap between a permitted conversion and an identical unpermitted one is the single largest avoidable value difference in this category.

Selling a home in probate or from a trust

The most expensive thing an estate can do with a house in Hayward is nothing, for several months, while the family decides. At $7,938 a month in carry, that is $47,630 over half a year, and the statutory fee structure does not shrink while they wait.

The structural fact that drives everything in California is that statutory probate fees are calculated on the gross value of the estate's assets, not on the family's equity. California Probate Code section 10810 sets the attorney fee schedule, and section 10800 applies the same schedule to the personal representative. The schedule runs 4 percent on the first 100,000 dollars of gross value, 3 percent on the next 100,000, 2 percent on the next 800,000, and 1 percent on the next 9 million. The mortgage does not reduce the calculation. A home worth a great deal with very little equity still generates fees on the full value.

Run it on this neighborhood. A home at the typical $816,424 generates a statutory attorney fee of about $19,328 under that schedule, and the personal representative is entitled to the same amount under section 10800, for a combined statutory cost of roughly $38,657 before court filing fees, probate referee fees, publication costs or any of the secondary expenses. If the home carries a large mortgage, that figure does not move. It is calculated on the $816,424, not on what is left after the bank.

Assembly Bill 2016 changed the picture for smaller estates. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, the revised Probate Code section 13150 allows a streamlined petition to transfer a decedent's primary residence valued up to 750,000 dollars without a full probate. The statutory fee schedule does not apply. The threshold applies to the primary residence only, and non-residential real property remains subject to a far lower threshold under section 13200. For Hayward specifically, this is the part that does not help. The typical home here is $816,424. That is $66,424 above the 750,000 dollar threshold, so the streamlined petition does not reach the typical property in this neighborhood. It does reach homes in the lower-value neighborhoods in the ranking table above, and it reaches a meaningful share of the housing stock in the inland parts of this region. That is worth knowing for a family with property in more than one area.

What are property taxes in Hayward?

I use 1.42 percent in this report, the Alameda County effective rate. On a home at $816,424 that is roughly $966 a month, $11,593 a year. What first-time buyer help is available in Hayward?

CalHFA runs the statewide programs and they layer on top of a first mortgage and do not replace it. MyHome is a deferred-payment junior loan toward the down payment and closing costs, open year round. ZIP covers closing costs on a CalHFA conventional first. Dream For All is the shared-appreciation program, limited to first-generation buyers, and it runs in announced windows, not continuously. GSFA Platinum is a separate option, and Hayward and Alameda County run their own programs on their own funding cycles. Those are the ones most people never hear about. The constraint that actually binds in a neighborhood at $816,424 is almost never income. It is the program's own purchase price cap, often set below the county loan limit. I would establish the price a program allows first, then shop inside it. Doing it the other way around is how people fall for a house the assistance cannot reach.

Can I build an ADU in Hayward?

Almost certainly yes, in some form. State law requires local agencies to allow accessory dwelling units on residential lots with ministerial approval and a decision deadline, so the real question is what size and configuration the local ordinance permits, not whether you are allowed. Owner-occupancy requirements on new ADUs have been removed, so the main house and the ADU can both be rented. Selling one separately is a different question. Assembly Bill 1033 lets a city allow an ADU to be sold as its own condominium unit, but it is permissive and not mandatory, relatively few cities have adopted an enabling ordinance, and without one there is no right to do it. That is a question for the Hayward planning department and the municipal code, not something the state law answers on its own.

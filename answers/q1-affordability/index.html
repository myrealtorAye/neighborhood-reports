How Much House Can You Afford in the East Bay?

The honest answer is that your real number comes from a licensed lender, not from a calculator and not from me. I'm a REALTOR, not a mortgage professional, so what follows is the general method lenders use and what it looks like against East Bay prices today. Your own figure depends on your income, your debts, your credit, your down payment, and the rate you actually get.

The rule lenders start with

Most lenders size a loan against two ratios, often called the 28/36 rule. No more than about 28 percent of your gross monthly income goes to the house payment, and no more than about 36 percent goes to all your debt combined, the house plus car loans, student loans, and credit cards. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lays this out plainly, and it is the same frame a loan officer will use when they pre-approve you.

The "house payment" in that math isn't just principal and interest. It's PITI: principal, interest, property taxes, and insurance, plus HOA dues if the home has them. People budget for the first two and forget the second two, and in California the taxes aren't small.

What that looks like against East Bay prices

Here is the math on a real number. The median asking price in Alameda right now is about $972,000. Put 20 percent down and you're financing roughly $778,000. At a 30-year fixed rate near 7.4 percent, the Freddie Mac average in mid-October 2026, that is about $5,390 a month in principal and interest.

Then add the parts people forget. California property tax runs a little over 1 percent of the purchase price for a new buyer, so call it around $970 a month here. Homeowners insurance adds roughly $150 a month. You're at about $6,500 a month before any HOA.

Run that back through the 28 percent rule and you need somewhere around $280,000 in household income to carry it comfortably. The median East Bay household earns about half of that. That gap is the real affordability question in this market, and pretending it away helps no one.

What actually moves your number

The $280,000 figure isn't a wall. It's one price point, one down payment, and one rate. Change any of them and the math changes:

  • A lower price point. Plenty of the East Bay and the Central Valley sits well under the Alameda median. The payment scales with the price.
  • The rate. Rates move, and they move the payment more than most people expect. The number above is a snapshot, not a promise, and it's why you check the current rate with a lender before you fall in love with a listing.
  • Your down payment, and whether you need 20 percent. You usually don't. FHA loans through HUD allow as little as 3.5 percent down, and conventional loans go lower than 20 for many buyers. Less down means a bigger loan and usually mortgage insurance, so a lender runs the tradeoff with you.
  • Down payment help. California runs real programs. CalHFA offers down payment and closing cost assistance for first-time and moderate-income buyers, and it's worth checking before you assume you're priced out.
  • The loan limit. Above a certain loan size you cross from a conforming loan into a jumbo, which can price differently. In the high-cost Bay Area counties the conforming ceiling is set higher than the national baseline; the FHFA publishes the exact figure by county each year.

Your next step

A calculator gives you a guess. A licensed lender gives you a pre-approval. That's the number sellers and I both take seriously, and it costs you nothing to get. Get pre-approved first, then we look at homes that fit it, not the other way around.

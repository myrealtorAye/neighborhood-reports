What Are Closing Costs for a Buyer in California?

Closing costs are the fees it takes to actually fund your loan and record the sale, and they land on top of your down payment. For a buyer in California they usually run about 2 to 5 percent of the purchase price. On a $900,000 home that's roughly $18,000 to $45,000, separate from whatever you put down.

What you're actually paying for

The list is long but it falls into a few buckets:

  • Loan fees. Origination, underwriting, and often points if you buy the rate down. Your appraisal falls here too.
  • Title and escrow. Title insurance protects you and your lender against claims on the property, and the escrow company handles the money and the paperwork. In California these are real line items, and in many areas buyer and seller split escrow by local custom.
  • Prepaids and reserves. The lender collects some property tax and homeowners insurance up front and sets up an impound account, so part of what feels like a "fee" is really your own future taxes and insurance paid early.
  • Recording and government fees. The county charges to record the new deed.

One California note: the city and county documentary transfer tax is often paid by the seller by custom, but allocation is set in the purchase contract and it's negotiable, so it's worth knowing which side is carrying it before you sign.

How to see your real number

You don't have to guess. By federal law your lender gives you a Loan Estimate within three business days of your application, and a Closing Disclosure before you close, both on standard forms from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Those two documents are the honest, itemized answer for your exact loan, and comparing Loan Estimates from more than one lender is how you catch a bad deal.

Your next step

Before you're deep in a deal, a lender can give you a rough closing cost estimate alongside your pre-approval, and escrow firms it up once you're in contract. I'll make sure that number is on the table early so it isn't a surprise at the end.

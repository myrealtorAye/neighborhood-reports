What Credit Score Do You Need to Buy a House?

There's no single magic number, and anyone who gives you one is rounding off the truth. Different loans have different floors, and your score does two jobs: it decides whether you qualify at all, and it moves the rate you're offered, which moves your payment for the life of the loan.

The rough floors by loan type

  • FHA loans can go as low as a 580 score with 3.5 percent down, and some lenders go to 500 with 10 percent down.
  • Conventional loans backed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac typically start around 620.
  • VA and USDA loans don't set a hard federal minimum, but individual lenders usually want the low-to-mid 600s.

Those are floors, not targets. The better your score, the better the rate, and the gap is real money. A buyer in the 740-plus range is usually offered a noticeably lower rate than a buyer at 640, and over 30 years that difference adds up.

What actually goes into it

Your score is built mostly from paying on time and from how much of your available credit you're using. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau publishes straight guidance on both. If your score isn't where you want it, the fixes are usually boring and effective: pay every bill on time, bring down card balances, and don't open or close a bunch of accounts right before you apply. A HUD-approved housing counselor can help you build a plan, often for free.

Don't guess, get it pulled

The worst move is to assume you won't qualify and never ask. A lender pulls your actual score, tells you which loans you fit, and shows you what a few points of improvement would do to your rate. I'm not a lender and I can't advise you on credit, so that conversation is theirs, and it costs you nothing.

Warm Springs, Fremont: the October 2026 read

Leland Stanford bought a resort here in 1869 and his brother turned it into a winery that held half a million gallons.

Josiah Stanford ran the operation and built the vineyard out to 350 acres, with winery capacity around 500,000 gallons. The site is California Historical Landmark 642. Most of that ground is housing now and a single brick building is what is left. That is the usual ending for Bay Area agriculture, though not usually this close to a BART platform. The Weibel name on the neighborhood immediately north is the next chapter of the same story, a champagne operation that worked the Stanford site after the Stanfords.

The other thing about Warm Springs is the plant at the south end of Fremont. General Motors broke ground on Fremont Assembly in September 1961 and the first car came off the line in May 1963. GM closed it in 1982. It reopened in 1984 as NUMMI, a joint venture between GM and Toyota that rehired about 85 percent of the laid off workforce and then produced more than eight million vehicles, peaking at 428,633 in 2006. Toyota shut it in April 2010 and roughly 4,700 people lost their jobs. Tesla bought it at a steep discount and reopened it that October. In 2023 it built close to 560,000 vehicles and employed more than twenty thousand people.

That matters here in a way it does not matter in most neighborhoods. Two large employers inside two decades, with a hard stop in between, is the local economic history, and the housing demand in south Fremont tracks it.

What to do with that if you are buying. Warm Springs has the newest housing stock in this part of the city and a BART station, so you are competing with buyers who are pricing the commute more than the house. Check the year built, check whether the unit is in an HOA and what the dues cover, and get the assessment history, because a lot of the newer construction here sits inside districts that carry additional levies on top of the base tax rate. Pull the tax bill on any specific address before you decide what you can afford. The difference between two otherwise comparable homes here is often on that bill and not in the listing.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Fremont I cover, and what each one is:

  • Ardenwood. A Pennsylvania farmer named George Washington Patterson came to California for gold in 1849, found almost none, and in 1857 built a redwood farmhouse here instead.
  • Baylands. This is the edge of Fremont where the city stops and the bay starts, and most of it is federally protected.
  • Brookvale. Brookvale is one of the Fremont neighborhoods where the published history is genuinely thin, so this is the development record and the practical read, not a story somebody made up.
  • Cabrillo. Cabrillo is a 1960s tract neighborhood in north Fremont, and the useful facts about it are in the development record instead of in any story.
  • Centerville. There is a cemetery at Post Street and Bonde Way that is older than the city around it by a century.
  • Cherry Guardino. Cherry-Guardino takes its name from its two main streets, and like several Fremont tract neighborhoods it has almost no published history.
  • City Center. On September 6, 1960 the Fremont City Council picked the Fudenna ranch as the site for its civic center.
  • Glenmoor. Glenmoor has a homeowners association that was incorporated in March 1953, when there were no more than 75 houses standing in the whole subdivision.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,711,399 here, transfer tax runs about $1,883 at 1.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $941 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Emeryville at 26.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $42,785.

Eastmont, Oakland: the October 2026 read

General Motors built cars here for about fifty years, then left, and the plant became a shopping mall, and the mall became a social services centre.

Chevrolet put up an assembly plant on Foothill Boulevard in the mid 1910s, at 69th Avenue by some accounts and 73rd by others. Through the Second World War it stopped making cars and produced aircraft engines, ammunition and guns instead. When GM decided it wanted a modern facility it chose not to upgrade this one, built a new plant about seventeen miles south in Fremont, and closed Oakland Assembly in 1963. That Fremont plant later became NUMMI and is now the Tesla factory, so one corporate decision in the early 1960s shaped both this neighborhood and Warm Springs.

The site sold to real estate investors in 1964. The plant came down and Eastmont Mall went up on it, designed by the architect William Pereira. The first stores, anchored by a Safeway, opened on May 18, 1966, the rest followed in 1967, and the official grand opening was in November 1970. The mall declined after its J.C. Penney closed in June 1991. It was renovated into Eastmont Town Center, still privately owned, now built around leasing space to social service providers and community organisations.

So this neighborhood has been remade three times in a century by decisions made somewhere else, and that is the honest frame for buying here.

The housing is mostly postwar, built around the plant and then around the mall. Oakland has 191,713 housing units and 46.6 percent predate 1950, so Eastmont sits on the newer side of the city's stock.

Confirm the legal unit count with the city on any building, and read Oakland's rent adjustment program and the just cause rules before you model income.

On the house itself, ask the age of the roof, the supply lines, the panel and the furnace. That is the right list for postwar stock. And on any parcel inside the old plant footprint, ask directly for the environmental record and get it in writing before your contingencies expire. A sixty year automotive plant leaves a paper trail and you want to read it.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Oakland I cover, and what each one is:

  • Acorn. Fifty blocks of this neighborhood were demolished.
  • Adams Point. Adams Point is named for Edson Adams, one of the men who got hold of Oakland's land early, and in 1906 it filled up with San Franciscans who had nowhere else to go.
  • Allendale. The Allendale tract map was recorded in 1892, and there was a cinema operating here by 1913.
  • Brookfield Village. Twelve hundred houses went up here during the Second World War and they sold for four thousand dollars each.
  • Bushrod. Bushrod was annexed by Oakland in 1897, in the same batch as Temescal, Golden Gate and Piedmont Avenue, and that date is the most solid thing in its published record.
  • Caballo Hills. Caballo Hills is a hillside pocket at the southeastern edge of Oakland with very little published history, so this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Castlemont. Castlemont takes its name from the high school at its centre, and its published history is thin enough that this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Chabot Park. Anthony Chabot dammed a creek in 1868 and built Oakland its first water supply at municipal scale, and his name is on the parkland at the edge of this neighborhood.

What it costs to own here

Carry on the typical home here is roughly $4,897 monthly, or $58,761 annually. That covers the loan payment, property tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance at a stated budget. HOA dues are excluded, because they differ building to building.

The 30-year fixed average is 7.4 percent as of 2026-10-15, against 6.27 percent in the same week last year. The conforming and FHA ceiling for a one-unit property in Alameda County is $1,249,125 in 2026.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Transfer tax on the typical Eastmont home, $454,122, comes to roughly $7,311 at a rate of 16.10 per thousand. It is normally split down the middle, about $3,656 on each party. The contract governs, not the custom.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $2,000,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 18.60 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $5,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $2,000,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $6,812.

What waiting costs

Hold off two years and the combined price and payment cost is near -$44,564. At five years it reaches -$126,094.

Treat it as a scenario, not a prediction. The appreciation input is one year of observed change carried forward, and five years of compounding on one year of data flatters the result.

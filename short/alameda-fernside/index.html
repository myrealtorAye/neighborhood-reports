Fernside, Alameda: the October 2026 read

There was a seventy room mansion on Fernside Boulevard and it burned to the ground in 1897 with nobody in it.

It belonged to Alfred A. Cohen, and the money that built it came from the railroad. Cohen built the San Francisco and Alameda Railroad in 1864, the same line whose Alameda stop created the commercial district at Lincoln and Park. In 1868 he sold his railroads and his ferries to the Central Pacific's Big Four, and he put the proceeds into an estate here on the east end of the island. Sources put the estate at somewhere between about 102 and 250 acres. The house went up in flames in March 1897. Accounts differ on whether it was the twenty third or the twenty fourth, and one source puts the fire a year later, though 1897 is the better supported date.

The neighborhood came out of the estate's breakup. Emilie Cohen died in 1924, her children sold the land to a developer named Fred Wood, and Wood laid out the streets that are there now. A separate project, Fernside Marina, went in along the estuary in 1938 and is generally described as the last subdivision on Alameda's estuary shoreline.

So the housing here is 1920s and newer, Craftsman and bungalow through mid century, on a layout more generous than the old grid closer to town. A remnant of the original subdivision survives in the neighborhood's homeowners association, and that is unusual for ground of this age.

Fernside also runs along the tidal canal, the 1902 cut that severed this peninsula from Oakland and made Alameda an island. The Fruitvale and High Street bridges cross it here, and that is both the appeal and the thing to check. This is the shortest way off the island toward the freeway, and it is also the closest you can live to a drawbridge.

Three things before you write an offer. Confirm whether the address is inside the association and read what it governs, because an association tied to a 1920s subdivision can carry restrictions nobody raises at an open house. Pull the year built, since a 1920s bungalow and a 1950s house are different inspection problems entirely. And near the water, get the flood zone determination in writing with an actual insurance quote before your contingencies expire. On this side of the island that premium moves a monthly payment more than a quarter point of rate does.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Alameda I cover, and what each one is:

  • Alameda Point. This is 1,560 acres of the island that belonged to the United States Navy until 1997, and the Secretary of the Interior made the heart of it a National Historic Landmark District in January 2013.
  • Ballena Bay. Ballena Bay is an island off the side of an island, and a good part of it runs on a lease from the city.
  • Bay Farm. A man named Amos Mecartney tried to farm this ground in the 1870s and it did not work out.
  • Bronze Coast. The Bronze Coast sits between the Gold Coast and the rest of the island, and the name tells you exactly what it is: the same era, a step down in scale.
  • Central Alameda. Central Alameda is the part of the island everybody drives through and nobody can draw the boundary of.
  • Gold Coast. The name is not marketing.
  • Harbor Bay Isle. Harbor Bay Isle was supposed to be ten thousand homes.
  • Harbor Bay. Harbor Bay is the half of the Bay Farm fill that was built for work instead of for living, and the ferry is the reason anyone commutes from it.

What it costs to own here

Carry on the typical home here is roughly $13,663 monthly, or $163,957 annually. That covers the loan payment, property tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance at a stated budget. HOA dues are excluded, because they differ building to building.

The 30-year fixed average is 7.4 percent as of 2026-10-15, against 6.27 percent in the same week last year. In Alameda County the 2026 one-unit limit is $1,249,125. It serves as the conforming conventional ceiling and the FHA ceiling at once.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,474,580 here, transfer tax runs about $19,317 at 13.10 dollars per thousand. Local practice splits it down the middle, roughly $9,658 each, though the contract decides.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $22,119.

What waiting costs

Price and payment together, a two year wait here runs about $275,889. Stretch it to five and the figure is $936,050.

One year of data, extended. That is worth saying plainly, because compounding it out to five years makes the total look more certain than it is.

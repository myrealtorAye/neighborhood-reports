Park Street District, Alameda: the October 2026 read

The reason downtown Alameda is here is that a train stopped here in 1864.

The San Francisco and Alameda Railroad put its Alameda stop at what is now Lincoln Avenue and Park Street, and the commercial district grew up around it. Between 1877 and 1880 more than thirty new commercial buildings went up. The National Register listed the Park Street Historic Commercial District on May 12, 1982. Sources disagree about the district's acreage, so take the boundary from the city instead of from a summary.

The landmark on it is the Alameda Theatre at 2317 Central Avenue. Timothy Pflueger designed it, the same architect behind the Castro in San Francisco and the Paramount in Oakland, and it opened in 1932 with 2,168 seats and the largest projection screen on the West Coast at fifty feet by thirty. It went dark, served time as a roller rink and a gymnastics studio, and came back in 2008 after a $37.3 million project that restored the main theater and added a seven screen cineplex and a garage. It reopened on May 21, 2008.

This is the walkable commercial core of the island, and the 1997 closure of the naval air station at the far end of town pushed business toward Park Street at Webster Street's expense.

What that means if you are buying near here. The premium you pay is for the ability to do errands on foot, and it is real and it is durable. Verify it the obvious way: park the car and do a normal Saturday from the front door of the house you are considering. Groceries, coffee, a haircut, dinner. If it works, that is what you are paying for.

On the house itself, the stock near Park Street runs old and mixed, with single family, flats and small apartment buildings sometimes on the same block. Pull the year built and the permit history, and ask whether any unit count was added without permits. On this island that question has a long history.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Alameda I cover, and what each one is:

  • Alameda Point. This is 1,560 acres of the island that belonged to the United States Navy until 1997, and the Secretary of the Interior made the heart of it a National Historic Landmark District in January 2013.
  • Ballena Bay. Ballena Bay is an island off the side of an island, and a good part of it runs on a lease from the city.
  • Bay Farm. A man named Amos Mecartney tried to farm this ground in the 1870s and it did not work out.
  • Bronze Coast. The Bronze Coast sits between the Gold Coast and the rest of the island, and the name tells you exactly what it is: the same era, a step down in scale.
  • Central Alameda. Central Alameda is the part of the island everybody drives through and nobody can draw the boundary of.
  • Fernside. There was a seventy room mansion on Fernside Boulevard and it burned to the ground in 1897 with nobody in it.
  • Gold Coast. The name is not marketing.
  • Harbor Bay Isle. Harbor Bay Isle was supposed to be ten thousand homes.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Figure $15,011 in transfer tax on a $1,145,869 sale here, at 13.10 dollars per thousand. Local practice splits it down the middle, roughly $7,505 each, though the contract decides.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $17,188.

What waiting costs

Hold off two years and the combined price and payment cost is near $56,488. At five years it reaches $175,964.

That rate is one observed year extrapolated forward, not a forecast, and compounding a single strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. Weight the two year figure more heavily.

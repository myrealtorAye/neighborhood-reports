Montclair, Oakland: the October 2026 read

There is a sealed railway tunnel 3,700 feet long running under these hills, and its west portal is near the corner of Saroni Drive and Shepherd Canyon Road.

The Oakland and Antioch Railway dug the Redwood Peak Tunnel between 1910 and 1913 to carry trains under the Oakland hills toward Contra Costa County. The line that used it became part of the Sacramento Northern, and it ran right through Montclair Village and up Shepherd Canyon, crossing into Montclair on a trestle near Moraga Avenue and Thornhill Drive where a pocket park now marks the spot. Sources disagree on when service started, with 1911, 1912 and about 1913 all given, and passenger service ended in either 1940 or 1941. The last train went through in 1957 and the tunnel was sealed.

You can walk the right of way. The Montclair Railroad Trail runs from behind the Village up Shepherd Canyon with interpretive panels along it.

Montclair Village itself is the commercial centre of the Oakland hills, a genuine walkable district of small storefronts in a part of the city that is otherwise all car.

Then there is 1991. On the morning of Sunday, October 20, 1991 a grass fire that had been knocked down the day before near the Caldecott Tunnel came back to life shortly before eleven. Winds gusting to 65 miles an hour drove it down the south ridge of Claremont Canyon, and inside thirty minutes it had jumped Highway 24 and Highway 13 and set hundreds of houses alight. By the time it was fully controlled on October 23 it had killed 25 people, injured 150, and destroyed 2,843 single family homes and 437 apartment and condominium units. Losses ran to about 1.5 billion dollars. Cal Fire lists it as the Tunnel Fire and the cause was never determined. Montclair was among the neighborhoods where hundreds of houses burned, so the stock here is a mix of pre fire survivors and post 1991 rebuilds, sometimes on the same street.

So pull the year built first, because it tells you which of two completely different houses you are looking at.

Then fire insurance, in writing, on the exact address, before contingencies expire, with the risk rating explained. Ask about defensible space requirements and current compliance.

Then the hillside questions: soils and drainage history, retaining structures and who maintains them, and where water goes in a wet month. On any pre fire house, add foundation bolting and the sewer lateral Oakland certifies at sale.

What the market is doing right now

Typical home value in Montclair is $1,455,795 as of 2026-09-30, up 1.08 percent from the prior month and up 5.7 percent over the trailing year. Month over month is the number worth watching in a monthly series. A trailing twelve month figure keeps reporting last winter's market long after it has turned.

Listing activity in ZIP 94611 for 2026-09. Active inventory is 60, up 8.2 percent from the prior month and down 33.9 percent from a year earlier. 42 new listings came on, down 12.5 percent month over month, and 40 went pending. Median days on market is 22, down 20.4 percent month over month and down 50.3 percent year over year.

Median asking price is $1,089,750 at $542 per square foot, on a median listing of 1,943 square feet. Typical rent in the same area is $2,996, up 11.8 percent year over year.

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Montclair is up 5.7 percent year over year. The median asking price of what is actively listed in ZIP 94611 is down 0.7 percent year over year, and price per square foot is down 1.0 percent.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

What it costs to own here, month to month

Sellers underestimate this number, consistently, and it is the number that should set the pricing decision. Owning the typical home in Montclair runs about $472,405 a month, or $5,668,864 a year. That figure is the loan payment at 3.5 percent down and 400 percent, property tax at 1.71 percent, insurance at the California average, utilities for a home of roughly 1,800 square feet, and maintenance budgeted at 1 percent of value a year. HOA dues are not included, because they vary by building and subdivision.

On a loan of $1,404,842, the typical home here with the minimum down payment, that year of rate movement is the difference between a payment of $468,281 and $468,281, a change of $0 a month on the same house at the same price. Rate movement has been the larger force on affordability here than price movement.

This is the section most buyers in Montclair find out about too late. The 2026 one-unit loan limit in Alameda County is $1,249,125. That single figure is both the conforming conventional ceiling and the FHA ceiling. Above it, a loan is a jumbo, underwritten to a different and generally stricter standard, with its own reserve and credit requirements and no FHA option at all.

At the typical value here of $1,455,795, a 3.5 percent down payment leaves a loan of $1,404,842. That is $155,717 over the ceiling. To bring the loan down to the limit on a home at this value, the down payment has to be $206,670, or 14.2 percent. Put differently, the most expensive home a minimum-down FHA loan reaches in Alameda County is about $1,294,430, and the typical home in Montclair is $161,365 above that.

There are three honest paths from here, and they are worth knowing before touring anything. Bring the larger down payment and stay conforming. Take a jumbo and qualify under its standards. Or shop at or below $1,294,430. In Oakland that means looking at the neighborhoods further down the ranking table above, where the minimum-down programs still function. Each of those is a real strategy. What does not work is assuming the 3.5 percent down program applies at this price.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,455,795 here, transfer tax runs about $23,438 at 16.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $11,719 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $2,000,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 18.60 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $5,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $2,000,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $21,837.

What waiting actually costs here

This is the math on waiting, and the assumptions are stated, not buried. Appreciation is set at 5.7 percent, this neighborhood's own trailing twelve month change in typical value as of 2026-09-30. The rate is held at the current 400 percent average. The down payment is 3.5 percent. The formula is the price increase plus the payment increase over the waiting period, with rent shown separately and not folded into the headline figure.

At those assumptions, a home at $1,455,795 today becomes $1,627,663 in two years and $1,924,248 in five. The monthly payment moves from $468,281 to $523,565 and then $618,966. The cost of waiting comes to $1,498,691 over two years and $9,509,595 over five. Rent over those periods adds $71,906 and $179,766 on top, at a typical area rent of $2,996.

Now the part that most versions of this calculation leave out. Extrapolating 5.7 percent for five years is an extrapolation of a single observed year, not a forecast, and compounding one strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. It is worth seeing what the same math does on a more conservative input. At the citywide rate of 0.6 percent instead, the same home reaches $1,471,968 in two years and $1,496,565 in five. That is $427,683 less than the neighborhood-rate projection.

The cost of waiting is also not one-directional. Rates move, and they have moved more than prices here over the past year. Hold the price constant and move only the rate and the rate component shows up on its own. If rates fall, part of the cost of waiting reverses. If they rise, it compounds. Anyone presenting this calculation as a single certain number is selling, not calculating.

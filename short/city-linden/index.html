Linden: the October 2026 read

Linden is unincorporated San Joaquin County territory east of Stockton, in the cherry and walnut country. Stockton down the road was founded in 1849 by Captain Charles Maria Weber, who worked out that supplying the gold miners paid better than joining them, and the farm towns east of it grew to feed that trade.

Linden has 989 housing units and 6.5 percent of them predate 1950. 100.0 percent of them are single family detached, and that is the real figure, not a rounding. Owner occupancy runs 56.1 percent and the ACS median value is 600,100 dollars. ZHVI is about 677,000 dollars, up 3.7 percent over the year.

So the buying questions are mostly postwar questions.

Pull the year built and the permit history for the parcel. On a tract house of this era, ask about the electrical panel and its capacity, the supply lines, the sewer lateral, the roof, and whether the foundation has ever been bolted.

An original kitchen and original aluminium windows are either the discount or the asset. The inspection report tells you which.

Get the tax bill for the address, not the listing estimate.

Confirm first that the address is unincorporated San Joaquin County territory, because it changes who issues your permits, who provides your services, who polices it and how your tax bill is built. Take it from the county, not from a listing. Then ask which school district serves the parcel. Ask whether the parcel is on sewer or on septic, and if septic, get the most recent inspection and the pumping record. Ask whether water comes from a district or a well, and if a well, get the yield and the water quality tests in writing. On valley agricultural ground, also ask what is farmed on the adjoining parcels and when it is sprayed.

And one habit worth keeping in this county. Pull the year built before you form any opinion on a listing, because it decides the entire inspection list. Then get the tax bill for the exact address off the county roll, not the listing estimate, and read which districts and assessments are on it. In an unincorporated place there is no city hall between you and the county, so your permits, your inspections and your service districts all come from the county and each district levies on the parcel. Ask the county's Local Agency Formation Commission whether the parcel sits in any city's sphere of influence, because that is where future annexation would start and it is a public record.

What the market is doing right now

Typical home value in Linden is $676,713 as of 2026-09-30, up 0.27 percent from the prior month and up 3.7 percent over the trailing year. Month over month is the number worth watching in a monthly series. A trailing twelve month figure keeps reporting last winter's market long after it has turned.

Listing activity in Linden for 2026-09. Active inventory is 10, down 13.6 percent from the prior month and up 171.4 percent from a year earlier. 0 new listings came on, down 100.0 percent month over month, and 3 went pending. Median days on market is 53, up 47.2 percent month over month and down 13.1 percent year over year.

Median asking price is $799,625 at $385 per square foot, on a median listing of 2,161 square feet.

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Linden is up 3.7 percent year over year. The median asking price of what is actively listed in Linden is down 39.6 percent year over year, and price per square foot is down 17.2 percent.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

What waiting actually costs here

This is the math on waiting, and the assumptions are stated, not buried. Appreciation is set at 3.7 percent, this neighborhood's own trailing twelve month change in typical value as of 2026-09-30. The rate is held at the current 400 percent average. The down payment is 3.5 percent. The formula is the price increase plus the payment increase over the waiting period, with rent shown separately and not folded into the headline figure.

At those assumptions, a home at $676,713 today becomes $727,183 in two years and $810,032 in five. The monthly payment moves from $217,676 to $233,910 and then $260,560. The cost of waiting comes to $440,094 over two years and $2,706,362 over five.

Now the part that most versions of this calculation leave out. Extrapolating 3.7 percent for five years is an extrapolation of a single observed year, not a forecast, and compounding one strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. Treat the five year figure as a scenario built on one year of data, and weight the two year figure more heavily.

The cost of waiting is also not one-directional. Rates move, and they have moved more than prices here over the past year. Hold the price constant and move only the rate and the rate component shows up on its own. If rates fall, part of the cost of waiting reverses. If they rise, it compounds. Anyone presenting this calculation as a single certain number is selling, not calculating.

Fremont: the October 2026 read

Fremont is five towns that agreed to become one city on January 23, 1956, and it still reads that way on the ground.

Mission San Jose, Centerville, Niles, Irvington and Warm Springs each had their own main street, their own cemetery and their own reason for existing before the merger. Wally Pond, a former councilmember, is the one who got it through. Washington Township had organized the area back in 1853. Newark was asked to join, its voters declined, and it incorporated separately in 1955. The city took its name from John C. Fremont, who mapped a route through Mission Pass into the rest of the bay.

The result is a city that does not have one center. It has five, and the distance between them is why a buyer who has only looked at Fremont on a map tends to be surprised in person. Old Mission San Jose, at 43300 Mission Boulevard, was founded on June 11, 1797 and keeps the oldest standing adobe building in Alameda County, put up in 1809. Niles has a pre war commercial street and the studio ground where Charlie Chaplin shot five films in 1915. Warm Springs has what is left of the winery Leland Stanford bought in 1869 and his brother Josiah built out to 350 acres. Centerville has a cemetery from 1855 with the road names buried in it. Ardenwood has a redwood farmhouse from 1857 with an 1889 Queen Anne addition by Samuel Newsom.

The modern economy sits at the south end. General Motors opened Fremont Assembly in 1963, closed it in 1982, reopened it with Toyota as NUMMI in 1984, and that venture built more than eight million vehicles before Toyota shut it in April 2010 and 4,700 people lost their jobs. Tesla reopened it that October and built close to 560,000 vehicles there in 2023.

So treat Fremont as five housing markets instead of one. The year built swings from mission era ground to current construction, sometimes within a mile, and the inspection list, the tax bill and the commute are all different in each of the five. Pick the district first and the house second. And drive your actual commute, at your actual hour, before you decide which of the five you can live in.

Inside it

This report covers 19 areas of Fremont. What each one actually is:

  • Ardenwood. A Pennsylvania farmer named George Washington Patterson came to California for gold in 1849, found almost none, and in 1857 built a redwood farmhouse here instead.
  • Baylands. This is the edge of Fremont where the city stops and the bay starts, and most of it is federally protected.
  • Brookvale. Brookvale is one of the Fremont neighborhoods where the published history is genuinely thin, so this is the development record and the practical read, not a story somebody made up.
  • Cabrillo. Cabrillo is a 1960s tract neighborhood in north Fremont, and the useful facts about it are in the development record instead of in any story.
  • Centerville. There is a cemetery at Post Street and Bonde Way that is older than the city around it by a century.
  • Cherry Guardino. Cherry-Guardino takes its name from its two main streets, and like several Fremont tract neighborhoods it has almost no published history.
  • City Center. On September 6, 1960 the Fremont City Council picked the Fudenna ranch as the site for its civic center.
  • Glenmoor. Glenmoor has a homeowners association that was incorporated in March 1953, when there were no more than 75 houses standing in the whole subdivision.
  • Grimmer. Grimmer is named for a family nobody has documented properly, and the honest thing is to say so.
  • Irvington. In 1884 a man named Juan Gallegos built a winery here that was designed to work by gravity.
  • Kimber Gomes. This neighborhood is named after a chicken farm and a school board member, and the chicken farm was world famous.
  • Mission San Jose. The oldest standing adobe building in Alameda County is in this neighborhood, and it has been there since 1809.
  • Mission Valley. Mission Valley is the ground between the old mission and the valley floor, and it was built as housing tracts in the late 1960s.
  • Niles. Charlie Chaplin made five pictures on one block of Niles Boulevard, and you can still sit in a building there and watch them.
  • Northgate. Northgate is a north Fremont neighborhood with very little documented history of its own, so this is the development record and the practical read.
  • Parkmont. Parkmont is a postwar tract neighborhood in central Fremont, and what distinguishes it is the mix of what got built instead of any single story.
  • Sundale. Sundale has very little published history, so what follows is the development record and the buying read.
  • Warm Springs. Leland Stanford bought a resort here in 1869 and his brother turned it into a winery that held half a million gallons.
  • Weibel. The name on this neighborhood came off a wine label.

What it costs to own here

Owning that typical home runs about $13,361 a month, $160,327 a year, once the loan, the property tax, insurance, utilities and a maintenance budget are all counted. HOA dues are not in it, because they vary by building.

As of 2026-10-15 the 30-year fixed averages 7.4 percent, up or down from 6.27 percent twelve months before. The conforming and FHA ceiling for a one-unit property in Alameda County is $1,249,125 in 2026.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Transfer tax on the typical Fremont home, $1,439,805, comes to roughly $1,584 at a rate of 1.10 per thousand. Local practice splits it down the middle, roughly $792 each, though the contract decides.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $21,597.

What waiting costs

Waiting two years at this place's own trailing rate costs about -$49,092, counting the price increase and the payment increase together. Over five years it is -$144,921.

One year of data, extended. That is worth saying plainly, because compounding it out to five years makes the total look more certain than it is.

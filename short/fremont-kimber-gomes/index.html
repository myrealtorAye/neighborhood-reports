Kimber-Gomes, Fremont: the October 2026 read

This neighborhood is named after a chicken farm and a school board member, and the chicken farm was world famous.

John E. Kimber started Kimber Farms at Niles in 1925. The detailed company history puts the starting flock at 800 hens, though a local museum exhibit says 80,000, so take the smaller figure as the better documented one. Kimber was born in New York in 1895 and came out of Stanford in 1917 with a major in agriculture and a minor in music. The farm lost money for seven straight years and turned its first profit in 1932. Then it did something almost nobody in the industry was doing: it hired a full time geneticist, becoming only the second poultry breeder anywhere to employ one. Accounts differ on the year, with the company history saying 1934 and another source naming Dr W. F. Lamoreux in 1943. It also put a veterinarian on part time to autopsy every bird that died so the cause could be traced through each family line.

Kimber Farms incorporated on December 3, 1946, with Niles as headquarters and operations at Atascadero, Los Gatos, Sanger and Clovis. Its 1955 catalogue was about building a more profitable White Leghorn. By the 1940s through the 1960s the operation had international standing, and the USDA's National Agricultural Library holds its records today.

The Gomes half honors John Gomes, from a pioneer farming family that worked the fields nearby. He sat on the Irvington School Board for years and served as a director of the Alameda County Water District. John Gomes Elementary was built for the Way Out West and Mission Valley housing, took its first students in September 1969 and was dedicated in March 1970. The building is a six sided pod design and it still looks like nothing else around it.

So the housing here is late 1960s and after, built on former farm and ranch ground. That is the Fremont pattern. Only about 2 percent of the city's stock predates 1950.

Pull the year built and the lot dimensions. On single story houses of this era with generous lots, check setbacks early if an ADU is in your plan. Then go look at the Gomes pod building. You will not mistake it for anything else.

What the market is doing right now

Typical home value in Kimber-Gomes is $2,381,985 as of 2026-09-30, up 0.90 percent from the prior month and up 2.5 percent over the trailing year. Month over month is the number worth watching in a monthly series. A trailing twelve month figure keeps reporting last winter's market long after it has turned.

Listing activity in Fremont for 2026-09. Active inventory is 243. 128 new listings came on, and 107 went pending. Median days on market is 31.

Median asking price is $1,379,105 at $844 per square foot.

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Kimber-Gomes is up 2.5 percent year over year. Realtor.com does not publish year over year listing figures at this geography, so the comparison below rests on the value index and the current month's listings.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

Rate movement has been the larger force on affordability here than price movement.

This is the section most buyers in Kimber-Gomes find out about too late. The 2026 one-unit loan limit in Alameda County is $1,249,125. That single figure is both the conforming conventional ceiling and the FHA ceiling. Above it, a loan is a jumbo, underwritten to a different and generally stricter standard, with its own reserve and credit requirements and no FHA option at all.

At the typical value here of $2,381,985, a 3.5 percent down payment leaves a loan of $2,298,616. That is $1,049,491 over the ceiling. To bring the loan down to the limit on a home at this value, the down payment has to be $1,132,860, or 47.6 percent. Put differently, the most expensive home a minimum-down FHA loan reaches in Alameda County is about $1,294,430, and the typical home in Kimber-Gomes is $1,087,555 above that.

There are three honest paths from here, and they are worth knowing before touring anything. Bring the larger down payment and stay conforming. Take a jumbo and qualify under its standards. Or shop at or below $1,294,430. In Fremont that means looking at the neighborhoods further down the ranking table above, where the minimum-down programs still function. Each of those is a real strategy. What does not work is assuming the 3.5 percent down program applies at this price.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $2,381,985 here, transfer tax runs about $2,620 at 1.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $1,310 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Emeryville at 26.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $59,550.

What waiting actually costs here

This is the math on waiting, and the assumptions are stated, not buried. Appreciation is set at 2.5 percent, this neighborhood's own trailing twelve month change in typical value as of 2026-09-30. The rate is held at the current 400 percent average. The down payment is 3.5 percent. The formula is the price increase plus the payment increase over the waiting period, with rent shown separately and not folded into the headline figure.

At those assumptions, a home at $2,381,985 today becomes $2,502,716 in two years and $2,695,381 in five. The monthly payment moves from $766,205 to $805,040 and then $867,014. The cost of waiting comes to $1,052,768 over two years and $6,361,930 over five.

Now the part that most versions of this calculation leave out. Extrapolating 2.5 percent for five years is an extrapolation of a single observed year, not a forecast, and compounding one strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. It is worth seeing what the same math does on a more conservative input. At the citywide rate of -1.5 percent instead, the same home reaches $2,311,992 in two years and $2,210,840 in five. That is $484,541 less than the neighborhood-rate projection.

The cost of waiting is also not one-directional. Rates move, and they have moved more than prices here over the past year. Hold the price constant and move only the rate and the rate component shows up on its own. If rates fall, part of the cost of waiting reverses. If they rise, it compounds. Anyone presenting this calculation as a single certain number is selling, not calculating.

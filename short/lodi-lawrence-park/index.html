Lawrence Park, Lodi: the October 2026 read

Lawrence Park is an older Lodi neighborhood.

Lodi started as a railroad stop called Mokelumne Station, when the Central Pacific put a station here in 1869. The renaming is a genuine muddle. A city page says the Legislature changed the name in 1874, to stop the confusion with Mokelumne City and Mokelumne Hill. Britannica gives 1873 and suggests a racehorse of the day. A city records finding aid says the residents did it themselves in 1878. Another account credits Assembly Bill 639 and points at Lodi, Italy. Residents voted on incorporation in 1906 and the county Board of Supervisors declared Lodi incorporated on December 6 of that year.

The arch is the landmark. E. B. Brown built it in 1907 for the first Lodi Tokay Carnival, an event that exists today as the Lodi Grape Festival, and a city page calls it the oldest standing welcome arch in the United States. It was restored in 1956 after it had deteriorated into a hazard. The carnival was built around the Flame Tokay, a grape developed here, and the whole point of it in 1907 was to advertise that grape.

Lodi has 24,806 housing units and 15.9 percent of them predate 1950, the highest pre war share of any city in San Joaquin County. 64.9 percent are single family detached, owner occupancy runs 56.8 percent, and the ACS median value is 492,500 dollars. ZHVI is about 509,000 dollars, down 1.4 percent over the year.

So the first questions here are the old housing questions.

Pull the year built. On pre war stock, ask about foundation bolting, knob and tube wiring, galvanized supply lines, and the condition of the original windows and siding. Confirm the legal unit count with the city, not the listing, and get the permit history for the parcel.

Ask whether the building is nonconforming, because that limits what you can rebuild after a loss and it affects financing and insurance.

Get the tax bill for the address, not the listing estimate.

Then the part specific to this address. Pull the tract map for the subdivision from the county recorder. The recording date, the original lot dimensions and the approved street layout are all on it, and that record beats anything a listing will tell you about when and how this neighborhood was built.

Then drive your commute at the hour you would really drive it, and walk the block after dinner.

What the market is doing right now

Typical home value in Lawrence Park is $508,738 as of 2026-09-30, up 0.06 percent from the prior month and down 1.4 percent over the trailing year. Month over month is the number worth watching in a monthly series. A trailing twelve month figure keeps reporting last winter's market long after it has turned.

Median asking price is $596,863 at $333 per square foot.

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Lawrence Park is down 1.4 percent year over year. Realtor.com does not publish year over year listing figures at this geography, so the comparison below rests on the value index and the current month's listings.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

What waiting actually costs here

This is the math on waiting, and the assumptions are stated, not buried. Appreciation is set at -1.4 percent, this neighborhood's own trailing twelve month change in typical value as of 2026-09-30. The rate is held at the current 400 percent average. The down payment is 3.5 percent. The formula is the price increase plus the payment increase over the waiting period, with rent shown separately and not folded into the headline figure.

At those assumptions, a home at $508,738 today becomes $495,072 in two years and $475,260 in five. The monthly payment moves from $163,644 to $159,248 and then $152,875. The cost of waiting comes to -$119,159 over two years and -$679,593 over five.

Now the part that most versions of this calculation leave out. Extrapolating -1.4 percent for five years is an extrapolation of a single observed year, not a forecast, and compounding one strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. Treat the five year figure as a scenario built on one year of data, and weight the two year figure more heavily.

The cost of waiting is also not one-directional. Rates move, and they have moved more than prices here over the past year. Hold the price constant and move only the rate and the rate component shows up on its own. If rates fall, part of the cost of waiting reverses. If they rise, it compounds. Anyone presenting this calculation as a single certain number is selling, not calculating.

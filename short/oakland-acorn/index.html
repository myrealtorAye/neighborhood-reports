Acorn, Oakland: the October 2026 read

Fifty blocks of this neighborhood were demolished. That is the fact everything else here follows from.

By 1963 complete demolition of fifty blocks of Acorn was already under way as part of West Oakland's redevelopment programme. Acorn was one of five West Oakland neighborhoods that local authorities had designated for clearance. The adjacent Oak Center neighborhood organised against the same treatment and succeeded, and when the city wrote the Oak Center plan in 1970 its objectives had flipped to preserving Victorians and encouraging ownership. Acorn had already been cleared by then.

So what stands here now is largely what replaced what was removed: planned housing from the redevelopment era instead of the Victorian and Edwardian stock that characterises the West Oakland blocks that survived.

That makes Acorn unusual in a city where 46.6 percent of the 191,713 housing units predate 1950. Here the housing is substantially newer than the Oakland norm, and the inspection list that dominates the rest of West Oakland largely does not apply.

What does apply is different. On redevelopment era multi family housing, the questions are the roof, the plumbing risers, the electrical capacity, the windows and the mechanical systems, all of which reach end of life on a predictable schedule. Ask the age of each.

Confirm the ownership and management structure, because housing built in this period under redevelopment programmes can carry restrictions, ground leases or affordability covenants recorded against title. Ask the title company directly what is recorded, and ask the city whether any agreement limits resale, occupancy or rent. That is not a detail and it is not always volunteered.

If the property holds more than one unit, read Oakland's rent adjustment program and the just cause for eviction rules, then confirm with the city which of them apply to a building of this vintage, because the cut off dates matter.

Then walk the surrounding blocks. Acorn sits among neighborhoods that were not cleared, and the contrast between them, street by street, is the clearest physical record of what redevelopment did to West Oakland.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Oakland I cover, and what each one is:

  • Adams Point. Adams Point is named for Edson Adams, one of the men who got hold of Oakland's land early, and in 1906 it filled up with San Franciscans who had nowhere else to go.
  • Allendale. The Allendale tract map was recorded in 1892, and there was a cinema operating here by 1913.
  • Brookfield Village. Twelve hundred houses went up here during the Second World War and they sold for four thousand dollars each.
  • Bushrod. Bushrod was annexed by Oakland in 1897, in the same batch as Temescal, Golden Gate and Piedmont Avenue, and that date is the most solid thing in its published record.
  • Caballo Hills. Caballo Hills is a hillside pocket at the southeastern edge of Oakland with very little published history, so this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Castlemont. Castlemont takes its name from the high school at its centre, and its published history is thin enough that this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Chabot Park. Anthony Chabot dammed a creek in 1868 and built Oakland its first water supply at municipal scale, and his name is on the parkland at the edge of this neighborhood.
  • Chinatown. Oakland Chinatown is older than almost everything around it.

What it costs to own here

Put the minimum down on the typical Acorn home and the monthly carry lands near $6,868. That is $82,412 across a year, counting loan, tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance. HOA dues are not in it, since no two are alike.

The 30-year fixed average is 7.4 percent as of 2026-10-15, against 6.27 percent in the same week last year. The conforming and FHA ceiling for a one-unit property in Alameda County is $1,249,125 in 2026.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Acorn charges 16.10 per thousand, so the typical $674,588 home owes roughly $10,861 at transfer. It is normally split down the middle, about $5,430 on each party. The contract governs, not the custom.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $2,000,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 18.60 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $5,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $2,000,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $10,119.

What waiting costs

Waiting two years at this place's own trailing rate costs about -$45,215, counting the price increase and the payment increase together. Over five years it is -$130,614.

One year of data, extended. That is worth saying plainly, because compounding it out to five years makes the total look more certain than it is.

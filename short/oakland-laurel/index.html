Laurel, Oakland: the October 2026 read

This neighborhood was called Key Route Heights, after the streetcar system, until a school changed its name.

The first tract subdividing this land went in during 1900 and was called Laurel Grove Park. Oakland built Laurel Elementary School in 1910, and the Laurel name took over from Key Route Heights. The district was not part of Oakland at all until the 1909 annexation, the largest in the city's history. That same vote brought in Fruitvale, Elmhurst, Melrose and Fitchburg.

The street that made it was Hopkins, extended eastward with Key System streetcars running on it. Hopkins is now MacArthur Boulevard, and it is still the commercial spine of the Laurel.

Then the freeway. Interstate 580 went through in the 1960s and severed neighborhoods across Oakland, drawing a hard line roughly between the hills and the flats. The Laurel sits on that seam, and the character of the blocks changes noticeably as you cross it.

The housing is early twentieth century and fairly consistent: bungalows and small houses on a regular grid, with commercial and multi unit buildings along MacArthur. Oakland has 191,713 housing units, 46.6 percent of them built before 1950, with 41.5 percent single family detached and owner occupancy at 42.3 percent citywide.

That consistency helps. Where most of the stock went up inside a twenty year window, comparable sales genuinely compare and the price spread is largely condition.

So pull the year built and compare within the era. Confirm the legal unit count with the city on anything that might hold more than one household, and if it does, read Oakland's rent adjustment program and the just cause rules before you model income.

Then the pre war list: foundation bolting, knob and tube wiring, galvanized supply lines, the sewer lateral Oakland certifies at sale, and the permit history on every addition.

Then walk MacArthur from 35th to Fruitvale on a Saturday, and walk a block on each side of the freeway. Those two walks tell you most of what you need to know about where to buy here.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Oakland I cover, and what each one is:

  • Acorn. Fifty blocks of this neighborhood were demolished.
  • Adams Point. Adams Point is named for Edson Adams, one of the men who got hold of Oakland's land early, and in 1906 it filled up with San Franciscans who had nowhere else to go.
  • Allendale. The Allendale tract map was recorded in 1892, and there was a cinema operating here by 1913.
  • Brookfield Village. Twelve hundred houses went up here during the Second World War and they sold for four thousand dollars each.
  • Bushrod. Bushrod was annexed by Oakland in 1897, in the same batch as Temescal, Golden Gate and Piedmont Avenue, and that date is the most solid thing in its published record.
  • Caballo Hills. Caballo Hills is a hillside pocket at the southeastern edge of Oakland with very little published history, so this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Castlemont. Castlemont takes its name from the high school at its centre, and its published history is thin enough that this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Chabot Park. Anthony Chabot dammed a creek in 1868 and built Oakland its first water supply at municipal scale, and his name is on the parkland at the edge of this neighborhood.

What it costs to own here

Carry on the typical home here is roughly $6,073 monthly, or $72,882 annually. That covers the loan payment, property tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance at a stated budget. HOA dues are excluded, because they differ building to building.

The survey average for a 30-year fixed is 7.4 percent at 2026-10-15, compared with 6.27 percent a year back. In Alameda County the 2026 one-unit limit is $1,249,125. It serves as the conforming conventional ceiling and the FHA ceiling at once.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Laurel charges 16.10 per thousand, so the typical $585,749 home owes roughly $9,431 at transfer. Convention puts about $4,715 on each side of the table. That is custom, and it is negotiable.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $2,000,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 18.60 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $5,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $2,000,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $8,786.

What waiting costs

Price and payment together, a two year wait here runs about -$99,449. Stretch it to five and the figure is -$268,039.

The input is a single trailing year, projected. It is not a forecast, and the five year number is the one to distrust first.

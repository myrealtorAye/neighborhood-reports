Ballena Bay, Alameda: the October 2026 read

Ballena Bay is an island off the side of an island, and a good part of it runs on a lease from the city.

In 1979 Alameda leased city owned land here to Marina Operators Incorporated, and in 1982 the city approved assigning that lease to a limited partnership. The leasehold covers roughly 27 acres of water and about the same again of land. The lessee and its predecessors built and ran a marina for something like 500 recreational boats, with the offices, restaurants and parking that go with it. Pier 29 is out here. The local historical society is clear on the point that Ballena Bay is genuinely an island.

That structure is the thing to understand. Part of what makes this address what it is sits on leased public land with a term and conditions attached, instead of on fee simple ground that somebody simply owns.

So before you buy, read the lease situation. Ask specifically what land under and around the property is city owned and leased, what the remaining term is, and what happens at expiration. Ask whether any part of what you are buying, including parking, docks or common area, depends on that leasehold. Your title company and the city can both answer this and it is not a detail.

If a boat is part of the appeal, get the berth arrangements in writing separately from the house. A slip is not automatically attached to a residence and the terms vary.

Then the waterfront list, all of which applies here with force. Flood zone determination and a written insurance quote before your contingencies expire. Settlement and fill questions put directly to the inspector, since this is made ground. The condition and the ownership of any seawall, bulkhead or revetment the property sits behind, because that is the most expensive thing that can go wrong on a parcel like this and it is frequently a shared obligation.

And on anything in an association, read the reserve study, not the dues figure. On the water, reserves are what pay for the structures that keep the water out.

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Ballena Bay is up 1.8 percent year over year. Realtor.com does not publish year over year listing figures at this geography, so the comparison below rests on the value index and the current month's listings.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

What waiting actually costs here

This is the math on waiting, and the assumptions are stated, not buried. Appreciation is set at 1.8 percent, this neighborhood's own trailing twelve month change in typical value as of 2026-09-30. The rate is held at the current 400 percent average. The down payment is 3.5 percent. The formula is the price increase plus the payment increase over the waiting period, with rent shown separately and not folded into the headline figure.

At those assumptions, a home at $968,237 today becomes $1,002,921 in two years and $1,057,290 in five. The monthly payment moves from $311,450 to $322,606 and then $340,095. The cost of waiting comes to $302,443 over two years and $1,807,774 over five.

Now the part that most versions of this calculation leave out. Extrapolating 1.8 percent for five years is an extrapolation of a single observed year, not a forecast, and compounding one strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. It is worth seeing what the same math does on a more conservative input. At the citywide rate of 2.1 percent instead, the same home reaches $1,009,372 in two years and $1,074,374 in five. That is -$17,084 less than the neighborhood-rate projection.

The cost of waiting is also not one-directional. Rates move, and they have moved more than prices here over the past year. Hold the price constant and move only the rate and the rate component shows up on its own. If rates fall, part of the cost of waiting reverses. If they rise, it compounds. Anyone presenting this calculation as a single certain number is selling, not calculating.

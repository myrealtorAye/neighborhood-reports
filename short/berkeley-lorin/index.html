Lorin, Berkeley: the October 2026 read

Before Lorin was part of Berkeley it was its own settlement, and its train stop was a set of twelve wooden steps up to the tracks.

The stop sat at Adeline and Alcatraz on the Berkeley Branch line, built in 1876 and run first by the Central Pacific and later the Southern Pacific. Lorin was the last stop before Berkeley. Until sometime after 1900 it stayed a flag stop, meaning the train came only if you signalled it, and the twelve steps were how you got from the street up to the platform. The settlement was unincorporated and it voted to join the City of Berkeley. The year is genuinely disputed: the district's own historical marker says 1892, one local history says the early 1900s, and a heritage association essay puts the annexation of the adjacent Newbury Station area at 1891. Take the year from city records instead of from a summary.

The rail alignment is still the most visible thing here. Adeline Street runs on the old right of way, at an angle to the surrounding grid. Electric streetcars came through in 1891, the Key System ran its F Line down Adeline on tracks parallel to the steam railroad, and Santa Fe hauled freight along the street until 1979 before the tracks were pulled.

The building stock is a mix of nineteenth century houses and small commercial buildings along Adeline, with later infill around them.

Three things to establish on a specific address. Year built and permit history, because some of this is genuinely nineteenth century and most of it has been altered. The standard pre war inspection list, starting with whether the foundation has ever been bolted and whether knob and tube wiring or galvanized supply lines are still in service. And the legal unit count, confirmed with the city, on anything that looks like it holds more than one household.

Then walk Adeline from Alcatraz north. The angle of the street is the clearest piece of nineteenth century infrastructure you can see in Berkeley without a map.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Berkeley I cover, and what each one is:

  • Bateman. Nobody is entirely sure who Bateman was, and the neighborhood is named after him anyway.
  • Berkeley Hills. On September 17, 1923, a fire came over the ridge out of Wildcat Canyon and burned a large part of this area to the ground in a single afternoon.
  • Berkeley Marina. There is a pier here that used to run three and a half miles out into the bay.
  • Claremont. The Claremont Hotel could not legally serve a drink for decades, and the reason was a state law measured with a ruler.
  • Cragmont. Cragmont is named for the rock, and the rock is why the neighborhood looks the way it does.
  • Downtown Berkeley. Shattuck Avenue is wide and straight through downtown because it was a railroad before it was a street.
  • Elmwood. The oldest building in Elmwood's shopping district went up in 1907, one year after the San Francisco earthquake, and that timing explains how the neighborhood filled in.
  • Gilman District. Green Day's original graffiti is on a beam above the stage at 924 Gilman Street, from back when they were called Sweet Children.

What it costs to own here

Carry on the typical home here is roughly $13,384 monthly, or $160,606 annually. That covers the loan payment, property tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance at a stated budget. HOA dues are excluded, because they differ building to building.

As of 2026-10-15 the 30-year fixed averages 7.4 percent, up or down from 6.27 percent twelve months before. In Alameda County the 2026 one-unit limit is $1,249,125. It serves as the conforming conventional ceiling and the FHA ceiling at once.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,442,475 here, transfer tax runs about $23,224 at 16.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $11,612 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $1,600,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 26.10 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $16,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $1,600,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $21,637.

What waiting costs

Price and payment together, a two year wait here runs about $278,021. Stretch it to five and the figure is $946,506.

The input is a single trailing year, projected. It is not a forecast, and the five year number is the one to distrust first.

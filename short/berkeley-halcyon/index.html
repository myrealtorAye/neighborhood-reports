Halcyon, Berkeley: the October 2026 read

Halcyon Court used to be a parking lot. Hospital visitors circled it looking for free parking on a bare stretch of oily asphalt. The neighbors took it back and turned it into a park.

It started with a block party in 1992 in the middle of Halcyon Court. Somebody raised the idea that the lot could stay free of cars every day instead of just that afternoon, and the effort that followed produced Halcyon Commons. The park is there now, where the asphalt was. For a neighborhood of roughly 860 households that is a real piece of civic work, and it is the thing people here point to.

The neighborhood runs between Telegraph, Ashby, Adeline and Woolsey, in the South Berkeley flats with Alta Bates immediately north. The building stock is early twentieth century. Listing records on Halcyon Court itself show a house built in 1932 and a triplex from 1922, and that mix of single family houses alongside small multi unit buildings is the pattern across the neighborhood.

So you are buying into a flat, central, walkable pocket with a park the residents built themselves and a hospital at the top of it.

Four things to settle on a specific address. Confirm the legal unit count with the city, because single family houses and small multi unit buildings sit side by side here and what a listing calls a duplex is not always what the city has on record. If it is more than one unit, read Berkeley's rent stabilization rules before you model any income, since they are specific and they will apply.

Then pull the year built and the permit history, and on pre war construction ask directly about foundation bolting, knob and tube wiring and galvanized supply lines.

Then go look at the hospital side. Proximity to Alta Bates means traffic, parking pressure and sirens, and how much of that reaches a given block varies a lot within four streets. Stand at the address itself in the early morning and again in the evening. That is the one thing about this neighborhood you cannot learn from a listing.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Berkeley I cover, and what each one is:

  • Bateman. Nobody is entirely sure who Bateman was, and the neighborhood is named after him anyway.
  • Berkeley Hills. On September 17, 1923, a fire came over the ridge out of Wildcat Canyon and burned a large part of this area to the ground in a single afternoon.
  • Berkeley Marina. There is a pier here that used to run three and a half miles out into the bay.
  • Claremont. The Claremont Hotel could not legally serve a drink for decades, and the reason was a state law measured with a ruler.
  • Cragmont. Cragmont is named for the rock, and the rock is why the neighborhood looks the way it does.
  • Downtown Berkeley. Shattuck Avenue is wide and straight through downtown because it was a railroad before it was a street.
  • Elmwood. The oldest building in Elmwood's shopping district went up in 1907, one year after the San Francisco earthquake, and that timing explains how the neighborhood filled in.
  • Gilman District. Green Day's original graffiti is on a beam above the stage at 924 Gilman Street, from back when they were called Sweet Children.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,442,475 here, transfer tax runs about $23,224 at 16.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $11,612 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $1,600,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 26.10 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $16,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $1,600,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $21,637.

Central Alameda, Alameda: the October 2026 read

Central Alameda is the part of the island everybody drives through and nobody can draw the boundary of.

The sources genuinely disagree. One puts it between Park Street and Grand Avenue, bounded by the estuary and Central Avenue. Another sets Webster Street to the west, Grand Street to the east, Central and Encinal to the south and Lincoln to the north. A third treats it as the broad residential middle of the island between the West End and the East End instead of a discrete neighborhood at all. What they agree on is that Park Street separates it from the East End. Get the line from the city if it matters to your search.

What is not in dispute is the housing, and it is the island's core stock: late nineteenth and early twentieth century houses on a grid laid out before cars, with the Gold Coast's larger Victorians to the south and smaller Craftsman and bungalow blocks through the middle. The second big development wave came after the Alameda, Oakland and Piedmont electric streetcar arrived in 1892.

Then 1940 changed the building type. When the naval air station was commissioned on November 1, 1940 at the west end, Alameda had to house a military workforce fast, and larger houses through the central part of the island were cut into flats or replaced outright with apartment buildings. Many of those buildings predate the city charter rules that voters passed in 1973 banning new multifamily construction, tightened in 1991 with a density cap of one unit per 2,000 square feet of land. A repeal went on the ballot in 2020 and failed.

So the legal unit count is the first question on any purchase here, not the last. Get the permit history, confirm with the city that every unit is legal and not an unpermitted 1940s conversion, and ask whether the building is nonconforming, because nonconforming status limits what you can rebuild after a loss and it affects both financing and insurance.

Then run the pre war list: foundation bolting, wiring, supply lines. And compare a single family house against a converted one on the same street in one afternoon. The spread tells you what this market actually pays for.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Alameda I cover, and what each one is:

  • Alameda Point. This is 1,560 acres of the island that belonged to the United States Navy until 1997, and the Secretary of the Interior made the heart of it a National Historic Landmark District in January 2013.
  • Ballena Bay. Ballena Bay is an island off the side of an island, and a good part of it runs on a lease from the city.
  • Bay Farm. A man named Amos Mecartney tried to farm this ground in the 1870s and it did not work out.
  • Bronze Coast. The Bronze Coast sits between the Gold Coast and the rest of the island, and the name tells you exactly what it is: the same era, a step down in scale.
  • Fernside. There was a seventy room mansion on Fernside Boulevard and it burned to the ground in 1897 with nobody in it.
  • Gold Coast. The name is not marketing.
  • Harbor Bay Isle. Harbor Bay Isle was supposed to be ten thousand homes.
  • Harbor Bay. Harbor Bay is the half of the Bay Farm fill that was built for work instead of for living, and the ferry is the reason anyone commutes from it.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,145,869 here, transfer tax runs about $15,011 at 13.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $7,505 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $17,188.

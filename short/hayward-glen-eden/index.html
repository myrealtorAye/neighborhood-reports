Glen Eden, Hayward: the October 2026 read

Eden is the old name for this whole area. Hayward sat in Eden Township, and the name survives on neighborhoods like this one long after the township stopped meaning anything administratively.

What the township was is the agricultural district that made the city. From the later nineteenth century this was one of California's leading fruit growing areas, shipping cherries, apricots, peaches, tomatoes and potatoes out by rail. Hunt Brothers opened a cannery in 1895 that anchored the local economy through each season. By 1950 Hayward had about 14,000 residents and the nickname Apricot City. Then the war ended and the orchards went under housing.

Glen Eden is part of that postwar build out, in the southern part of the city.

Hayward has 52,243 housing units and only 9.1 percent of them predate 1950, so this is overwhelmingly a postwar city. 51.2 percent are single family detached, owner occupancy runs 58.0 percent, the ACS median value is 854,400 dollars and the median gross rent is 2,391. Here the stock is postwar single family on regular lots, and the consistency is the useful part: where most houses went up inside a narrow window, the comparable sales genuinely compare and the price spread is condition, not era.

So pull the year built and compare only within it, then put the effort into the inspection, because that is where the money is in a uniform tract.

The postwar list: the roof and its age, the supply lines, the electrical panel capacity if you plan to add load, the sewer lateral, the furnace and the water heater.

Then the fault. It applies across this city. Ask whether the house has ever been bolted to its foundation and ask the title company whether the parcel sits inside an Alquist-Priolo earthquake fault zone.

Then check the lot dimensions and setbacks if an accessory dwelling unit is any part of your plan, because single storey houses on regular lots are the best candidates and the setback is usually the binding constraint.

Then drive your commute at your real hour.

What the market is doing right now

Typical home value in Glen Eden is $832,781 as of 2026-09-30, up 0.34 percent from the prior month and down 1.7 percent over the trailing year. Month over month is the number worth watching in a monthly series. A trailing twelve month figure keeps reporting last winter's market long after it has turned.

Listing activity in Hayward for 2026-09. Active inventory is 194. 96 new listings came on, and 78 went pending. Median days on market is 42.

Median asking price is $829,716 at $553 per square foot.

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Glen Eden is down 1.7 percent year over year. Realtor.com does not publish year over year listing figures at this geography, so the comparison below rests on the value index and the current month's listings.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

What it costs to own here, month to month

Sellers underestimate this number, consistently, and it is the number that should set the pricing decision. Owning the typical home in Glen Eden runs about $8,081 a month, or $96,968 a year. That figure is the loan payment at 3.5 percent down and 7.4 percent, property tax at 1.42 percent, insurance at the California average, utilities for a home of roughly 1,800 square feet, and maintenance budgeted at 1 percent of value a year. HOA dues are not included, because they vary by building and subdivision.

The 30-year fixed average is 7.4 percent as of 2026-10-15, against 6.27 percent in the same week a year earlier, a move of up 1.13 percentage points. The 15-year average is 6.71 percent. These are the Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey averages, published weekly, and they are survey averages and not a quote.

On a loan of $803,634, the typical home here with the minimum down payment, that year of rate movement is the difference between a payment of $4,959 and $5,564, a change of $606 a month on the same house at the same price. Rate movement has been the larger force on affordability here than price movement.

The 2026 one-unit loan limit in Alameda County is $1,249,125, and that figure is both the conforming conventional ceiling and the FHA ceiling. At the typical value here of $832,781, a 3.5 percent down payment leaves a loan of $803,634, comfortably inside the limit. Both FHA and conforming conventional are live options at this price point. That is not true in every neighborhood in this county, and it widens the program list considerably.

The ceiling still sets a practical top end. The most expensive home a minimum-down loan reaches in this county is about $1,294,430. Above that, the options narrow to a larger down payment or a jumbo loan.

What waiting actually costs here

This is the math on waiting, and the assumptions are stated, not buried. Appreciation is set at -1.7 percent, this neighborhood's own trailing twelve month change in typical value as of 2026-09-30. The rate is held at the current 7.4 percent average. The down payment is 3.5 percent. The formula is the price increase plus the payment increase over the waiting period, with rent shown separately and not folded into the headline figure.

At those assumptions, a home at $832,781 today becomes $803,953 in two years and $762,570 in five. The monthly payment moves from $5,564 to $5,372 and then $5,095. The cost of waiting comes to -$33,452 over two years and -$98,359 over five.

Now the part that most versions of this calculation leave out. Extrapolating -1.7 percent for five years is an extrapolation of a single observed year, not a forecast, and compounding one strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. It is worth seeing what the same math does on a more conservative input. At the citywide rate of -2.4 percent instead, the same home reaches $793,527 in two years and $738,087 in five. That is $24,483 less than the neighborhood-rate projection.

The cost of waiting is also not one-directional. Rates move, and they have moved more than prices here over the past year. Hold the price constant and move only the rate and the rate component shows up on its own. If rates fall, part of the cost of waiting reverses. If they rise, it compounds. Anyone presenting this calculation as a single certain number is selling, not calculating.

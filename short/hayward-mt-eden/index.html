Mt Eden, Hayward: the October 2026 read

In 1918 a man named Zenjuro Shibata moved out from Oakland and started a nursery here. California law at the time barred him from holding the land in his own name, so the purchase was recorded in his eldest son Yoshio's.

Mt. Eden Nursery began on a marshy plot that flooded, growing vegetables and flowers. The first greenhouse went up that same year, 1918, planted in carnations. In the mid 1930s the family switched the main crop to roses. During the Second World War the Shibatas were removed to Tule Lake. The nursery came back afterward and kept operating into the 1980s, and the greenhouses were finally demolished in the 1990s.

What is left is the garden. The Shibata Garden at 25941 Industrial Boulevard is the surviving piece of the Mt. Eden Nursery Company, and it is on this neighborhood's ground.

Mt. Eden was its own township before Hayward grew around it, out on the flat western side of the city toward the bay. The land that grew flowers and vegetables is now a mix of housing and industrial ground.

Hayward has 52,243 housing units and only 9.1 percent of them predate 1950, so this is overwhelmingly a postwar city. 51.2 percent are single family detached, owner occupancy runs 58.0 percent, the ACS median value is 854,400 dollars and the median gross rent is 2,391. Out here the stock runs later than the downtown average and industrial parcels sit close to housing.

So zoning is the first question. Pull the zoning for the parcel and for everything touching it and ask the city what is permitted next door, not what is operating there now.

Second, this is low flat ground near the bay on land that was historically marsh. Get the flood zone determination with a written insurance quote before your contingencies expire, and ask the inspector about settlement and about the foundation specifically.

Third, on any parcel with an industrial or nursery past, ask for the site history in writing.

Then go find the garden on Industrial Boulevard. A Japanese garden behind a warehouse district is not what anyone expects to find in Hayward.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Hayward I cover, and what each one is:

  • Burbank. Burbank is one of Hayward's older central neighborhoods, close in to downtown, and the published history belongs to the city, not the pocket, so this is that history plus the development record.
  • Downtown Hayward. The city was incorporated on March 11, 1876 under the name Haywards, with the s, and it was named after a hotel.
  • Eden Shores. Eden Shores is the newest ground in Hayward and it sits out on the bay edge.
  • Fairview Hills. Fairview Hills is on the unincorporated slope above Hayward, and that word unincorporated is the practical headline.
  • Fairway Park. Fairway Park is postwar Hayward out toward the bay side, laid out around a golf course, and the name is literal.
  • Glen Eden. Eden is the old name for this whole area.
  • Harder Tennyson. Harder and Tennyson are the two roads this neighborhood is named for, in the southern part of Hayward, and the history here is the city's.
  • Hayward Highland. Hayward Highland sits on the slope above the city with the Hayward Fault running along the foot of it.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $940,793 here, transfer tax runs about $9,032 at 9.60 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $4,516 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $14,112.

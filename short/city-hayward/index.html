Hayward: the October 2026 read

Hayward called itself the Apricot City, and it meant it.

The town incorporated on March 11, 1876 under the name Haywards, with an s, taken from the hotel the settlement had grown around. What made it was fruit. From the second half of the nineteenth century this was one of California's leading fruit growing districts, sending cherries, apricots, peaches, tomatoes and potatoes out by rail. Hunt Brothers opened a cannery here in 1895 and ran it as a seasonal industry large enough to build worker cottages and community buildings around itself. By 1950 the city had about 14,000 residents.

Two other stories sit in that landscape. In 1918 Zenjuro Shibata moved out from Oakland and founded Mt. Eden Nursery on a marshy plot, recorded in his eldest son Yoshio's name because California law then barred him from holding it in his own. The first greenhouse went up that year in carnations and the family switched to roses in the mid 1930s. The nursery ran into the 1980s and the Shibata Garden at 25941 Industrial Boulevard is what remains of it. And out on the bay flats there was a separate community called Russell City, named in the 1850s or laid out in 1907 depending on which account you take. The county removed it by eminent domain by 1964 and put an industrial park on the ground.

After the Second World War the orchards went under tracts, and that is the city you see now. Hayward has 52,243 housing units and only 9.1 percent of them predate 1950, so this is overwhelmingly a postwar place. 51.2 percent are single family detached, owner occupancy is 58.0 percent, the ACS median value is 854,400 dollars and the median gross rent is 2,391.

One thing applies everywhere here and nowhere else so strongly. The Hayward Fault runs the length of this city. Ask the title company whether any parcel you are serious about sits inside an Alquist-Priolo earthquake fault zone, and ask whether the house has ever been bolted to its foundation. In Hayward that is the first question, not the last.

Inside it

This report covers 18 areas of Hayward. What each one actually is:

  • Burbank. Burbank is one of Hayward's older central neighborhoods, close in to downtown, and the published history belongs to the city, not the pocket, so this is that history plus the development record.
  • Downtown Hayward. The city was incorporated on March 11, 1876 under the name Haywards, with the s, and it was named after a hotel.
  • Eden Shores. Eden Shores is the newest ground in Hayward and it sits out on the bay edge.
  • Fairview Hills. Fairview Hills is on the unincorporated slope above Hayward, and that word unincorporated is the practical headline.
  • Fairway Park. Fairway Park is postwar Hayward out toward the bay side, laid out around a golf course, and the name is literal.
  • Glen Eden. Eden is the old name for this whole area.
  • Harder Tennyson. Harder and Tennyson are the two roads this neighborhood is named for, in the southern part of Hayward, and the history here is the city's.
  • Hayward Highland. Hayward Highland sits on the slope above the city with the Hayward Fault running along the foot of it.
  • Hayward Hills. The Hayward Fault runs along the base of these hills, and it is the reason there are hills here at all.
  • Jackson Triangle. Jackson Triangle takes its name from its shape, wedged between Jackson Street and the streets that cut across it in central Hayward.
  • Longwood Winton Grove. Longwood and Winton Grove sit either side of Winton Avenue in central Hayward, and like most of this city the neighborhood's story is the city's story.
  • Mission Garin. Mission Boulevard runs along the foot of the hills here and Garin Regional Park is immediately above, and the neighborhood is named for both.
  • Mt Eden. In 1918 a man named Zenjuro Shibata moved out from Oakland and started a nursery here.
  • Prospect Hill. Prospect Hill is one of Hayward's older residential pockets on the rise just east of downtown, and the name describes exactly what it is.
  • Santa Clara. The Santa Clara neighborhood sits in central Hayward and its published history is the city's, so this is that plus the development record and the buying read.

  • The Cannery. The neighborhood is named after a cannery and the cannery is the reason Hayward existed as an economy.

What it costs to own here

Owning that typical home runs about $7,938 a month, $95,260 a year, once the loan, the property tax, insurance, utilities and a maintenance budget are all counted. HOA dues are not in it, because they vary by building.

Rates sit at 7.4 percent on the 30-year fixed as of 2026-10-15. A year earlier the same survey read 6.27 percent. Alameda County's 2026 one-unit loan limit sits at $1,249,125. That is the cap for conforming conventional and for FHA alike.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Figure $7,838 in transfer tax on a $816,424 sale here, at 9.60 dollars per thousand. It is normally split down the middle, about $3,919 on each party. The contract governs, not the custom.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $12,246.

What waiting costs

The two year cost of waiting, price plus payment, comes to about -$44,655 here. The five year figure is -$130,051.

One year of data, extended. That is worth saying plainly, because compounding it out to five years makes the total look more certain than it is.

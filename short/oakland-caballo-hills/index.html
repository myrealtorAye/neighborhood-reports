Caballo Hills, Oakland: the October 2026 read

Caballo Hills is a hillside pocket at the southeastern edge of Oakland with very little published history, so this is the development record and the buying read.

The ground is the story. These ridges carried the San Antonio redwood forest until it was logged out inside about fifteen years from the late 1840s. What stands now is second growth and mixed woodland, with regional parkland along the ridge and the city dropping away below.

The 1991 firestorm is the governing modern fact for everything on this side of Oakland. On the morning of Sunday, October 20, 1991 a grass fire that had been knocked down the day before near the Caldecott Tunnel came back to life shortly before eleven. Winds gusting to 65 miles an hour drove it down the south ridge of Claremont Canyon, and inside thirty minutes it had jumped Highway 24 and Highway 13 and set hundreds of houses alight. By the time it was fully controlled on October 23 it had killed 25 people, injured 150, and destroyed 2,843 single family homes and 437 apartment and condominium units. Losses ran to about 1.5 billion dollars. Cal Fire lists it as the Tunnel Fire and the cause was never determined. Even where a specific street was untouched, that event set how carriers price this entire ridge.

The housing is later single family on sloped lots, planned in layout, and a good deal of it sits inside homeowner associations. Oakland has 191,713 housing units and 46.6 percent predate 1950, but up here the stock is newer than the city average. Owner occupancy citywide is 42.3 percent and runs higher on this kind of ground.

So four things, in this order, before the house itself.

Fire insurance: a written quote on the exact address before your contingencies expire, with the risk rating explained, the defensible space requirement confirmed, and a straight answer on which carriers write this zone.

Homeowner association: get the budget and the reserve study, not the dues figure, and ask specifically what the association is responsible for on the slopes, because on hill ground that can include drainage and retaining structures.

Hillside: soils and drainage history for the parcel, the condition and ownership of any retaining structures, and where water leaves the property in a wet month.

Then the house, matched to its year built, and a drive in and out at your real commute hour after dark.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Oakland I cover, and what each one is:

  • Acorn. Fifty blocks of this neighborhood were demolished.
  • Adams Point. Adams Point is named for Edson Adams, one of the men who got hold of Oakland's land early, and in 1906 it filled up with San Franciscans who had nowhere else to go.
  • Allendale. The Allendale tract map was recorded in 1892, and there was a cinema operating here by 1913.
  • Brookfield Village. Twelve hundred houses went up here during the Second World War and they sold for four thousand dollars each.
  • Bushrod. Bushrod was annexed by Oakland in 1897, in the same batch as Temescal, Golden Gate and Piedmont Avenue, and that date is the most solid thing in its published record.
  • Castlemont. Castlemont takes its name from the high school at its centre, and its published history is thin enough that this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Chabot Park. Anthony Chabot dammed a creek in 1868 and built Oakland its first water supply at municipal scale, and his name is on the parkland at the edge of this neighborhood.
  • Chinatown. Oakland Chinatown is older than almost everything around it.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $964,107 here, transfer tax runs about $15,522 at 16.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $7,761 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $2,000,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 18.60 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $5,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $2,000,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $14,462.

Coronado, Richmond: the October 2026 read

Coronado is one of Richmond's older flatland neighborhoods, south of the city centre.

Standard Oil built its refinery here in 1901, with one account giving 1902. By 1904 it was reported as the second largest oil refinery in the world. Chevron runs it today. The city incorporated in 1905, after the refinery, not before it.

Henry Kaiser opened his first Richmond shipyard in December 1940. The city had fewer than 24,000 people that year and nearly 100,000 by 1943. Four yards here built 747 ships, more than any other shipyard complex in the country. The Rosie the Riveter World War II Home Front National Historical Park was dedicated on October 25, 2000 and was the first national park created to commemorate the home front.

Richmond has 42,509 housing units and 28.5 percent of them predate 1950. 59.9 percent are single family detached, owner occupancy runs 54.7 percent, and the ACS median value is 668,200 dollars.

So the first questions here are the old housing questions.

Pull the year built. Confirm the legal unit count with the city, not the listing, and get the permit history. Ask whether the building is nonconforming, because that limits what you can rebuild after a loss and it affects financing and insurance.

On pre war stock: foundation bolting, knob and tube wiring, galvanized supply lines, and the condition of original windows and siding.

Ask for the site history in writing on any parcel with an industrial past. Richmond has a century of heavy industry in it and the record is public.

Pull the zoning for the parcel and everything touching it. Richmond has heavy industry woven through its flats and a permitted use next door is not always what operates there now.

One Richmond specific worth knowing. The city formally recognises 28 neighborhood councils and Coronado is one of them. The system came out of the upheaval the Kaiser shipyards caused here, with an early pilot in North Richmond in 1956. The Richmond Neighborhood Coordinating Council is the umbrella body for all of them and it is the final arbiter of where one council's boundary ends and the next begins. It meets the second Monday of the month at 7:00 pm.

So take the boundary from the city's own neighborhood council map, not from a listing. Agents draw these lines loosely and the city's map is the one that governs. Then sit in on a council meeting before you write an offer. That hour tells you what the neighborhood is arguing about, and no listing will.

Then drive your commute at the hour you would really drive it, and walk the block after dinner.

What the market is doing right now

Typical home value in Coronado is $505,246 as of 2026-09-30, up 0.53 percent from the prior month and up 0.2 percent over the trailing year. Month over month is the number worth watching in a monthly series. A trailing twelve month figure keeps reporting last winter's market long after it has turned.

Listing activity in Richmond for 2026-09. Active inventory is 115. 54 new listings came on, and 66 went pending. Median days on market is 30.

Median asking price is $560,113 at $509 per square foot.

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Coronado is up 0.2 percent year over year. Realtor.com does not publish year over year listing figures at this geography, so the comparison below rests on the value index and the current month's listings.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

What it costs to own here, month to month

Sellers underestimate this number, consistently, and it is the number that should set the pricing decision. Owning the typical home in Coronado runs about $5,185 a month, or $62,225 a year. That figure is the loan payment at 3.5 percent down and 7.4 percent, property tax at 1.31 percent, insurance at the California average, utilities for a home of roughly 1,800 square feet, and maintenance budgeted at 1 percent of value a year. HOA dues are not included, because they vary by building and subdivision.

The 30-year fixed average is 7.4 percent as of 2026-10-15, against 6.27 percent in the same week a year earlier, a move of up 1.13 percentage points. The 15-year average is 6.71 percent. These are the Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey averages, published weekly, and they are survey averages and not a quote.

On a loan of $487,562, the typical home here with the minimum down payment, that year of rate movement is the difference between a payment of $3,008 and $3,376, a change of $367 a month on the same house at the same price. Rate movement has been the larger force on affordability here than price movement.

The 2026 one-unit loan limit in Contra Costa County is $1,249,125, and that figure is both the conforming conventional ceiling and the FHA ceiling. At the typical value here of $505,246, a 3.5 percent down payment leaves a loan of $487,562, comfortably inside the limit. Both FHA and conforming conventional are live options at this price point. That is not true in every neighborhood in this county, and it widens the program list considerably.

The ceiling still sets a practical top end. The most expensive home a minimum-down loan reaches in this county is about $1,294,430. Above that, the options narrow to a larger down payment or a jumbo loan.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $505,246 here, transfer tax runs about $4,092 at 8.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $2,046 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $1,000,000 the rate steps from 8.10 to 13.50 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $5,400 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $1,000,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Antioch charges 1.10 per thousand against El Cerrito at 13.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $6,063.

What waiting actually costs here

This is the math on waiting, and the assumptions are stated, not buried. Appreciation is set at 0.2 percent, this neighborhood's own trailing twelve month change in typical value as of 2026-09-30. The rate is held at the current 7.4 percent average. The down payment is 3.5 percent. The formula is the price increase plus the payment increase over the waiting period, with rent shown separately and not folded into the headline figure.

At those assumptions, a home at $505,246 today becomes $507,301 in two years and $510,400 in five. The monthly payment moves from $3,376 to $3,390 and then $3,410. The cost of waiting comes to $2,385 over two years and $7,221 over five.

Now the part that most versions of this calculation leave out. Extrapolating 0.2 percent for five years is an extrapolation of a single observed year, not a forecast, and compounding one strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. It is worth seeing what the same math does on a more conservative input. At the citywide rate of -1.0 percent instead, the same home reaches $495,303 in two years and $480,756 in five. That is $29,645 less than the neighborhood-rate projection.

The cost of waiting is also not one-directional. Rates move, and they have moved more than prices here over the past year. Hold the price constant and move only the rate and the rate component shows up on its own. If rates fall, part of the cost of waiting reverses. If they rise, it compounds. Anyone presenting this calculation as a single certain number is selling, not calculating.

North Berkeley, Berkeley: the October 2026 read

North Berkeley's housing stock has a hard line running through it, and the line is a single day in 1923.

On September 17 that year, a fire out of Wildcat Canyon crossed the ridge on a northeast wind and destroyed between 543 and 640 structures in this part of the city, about 585 of them houses. Roughly 4,000 people were put out of their homes and no one died. The water mains in the burn area were two inches in diameter. The hydrants were close to useless. The fire ran through La Loma Park and the Northside before the wind shifted and it stopped. The practical legacy is that a lot of the houses here date to the rebuild instead of to the original build.

Below the burn line, the neighborhood is the early twentieth century Berkeley that everyone pictures: brown shingle and Craftsman houses on a grid, large street trees, and a commercial cluster at Shattuck and Vine that people have called the Gourmet Ghetto for decades.

Where the land starts to climb, the rock parks begin. Mason-McDuffie donated the parcels around five volcanic outcrops to the city while developing this side of Berkeley, and they were dedicated as parks in 1917. Indian Rock and Mortar Rock are the two most people know, and the Ohlone bedrock mortars ground into Mortar Rock are still visible.

Two different buying problems, then, depending on which half you are in. On the flats, the questions are the pre war ones: foundation bolting, knob and tube wiring, galvanized supply lines, and the permit history on any addition. Up the hill, add fire insurance quoted in writing on the exact address, plus soils and drainage documentation on the parcel.

Either way, pull the year built first. In North Berkeley that single number tells you which side of 1923 you are on, and it changes the entire inspection conversation.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Berkeley I cover, and what each one is:

  • Bateman. Nobody is entirely sure who Bateman was, and the neighborhood is named after him anyway.
  • Berkeley Hills. On September 17, 1923, a fire came over the ridge out of Wildcat Canyon and burned a large part of this area to the ground in a single afternoon.
  • Berkeley Marina. There is a pier here that used to run three and a half miles out into the bay.
  • Claremont. The Claremont Hotel could not legally serve a drink for decades, and the reason was a state law measured with a ruler.
  • Cragmont. Cragmont is named for the rock, and the rock is why the neighborhood looks the way it does.
  • Downtown Berkeley. Shattuck Avenue is wide and straight through downtown because it was a railroad before it was a street.
  • Elmwood. The oldest building in Elmwood's shopping district went up in 1907, one year after the San Francisco earthquake, and that timing explains how the neighborhood filled in.
  • Gilman District. Green Day's original graffiti is on a beam above the stage at 924 Gilman Street, from back when they were called Sweet Children.

What it costs to own here

Carry on the typical home here is roughly $12,139 monthly, or $145,670 annually. That covers the loan payment, property tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance at a stated budget. HOA dues are excluded, because they differ building to building.

As of 2026-10-15 the 30-year fixed averages 7.4 percent, up or down from 6.27 percent twelve months before. The conforming and FHA ceiling for a one-unit property in Alameda County is $1,249,125 in 2026.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Transfer tax on the typical North Berkeley home, $1,299,377, comes to roughly $20,920 at a rate of 16.10 per thousand. Expect it split, near enough $10,460 apiece, with the contract having the final say.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $1,600,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 26.10 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $16,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $1,600,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $19,491.

What waiting costs

Price and payment together, a two year wait here runs about $317,544. Stretch it to five and the figure is $1,114,992.

Treat it as a scenario, not a prediction. The appreciation input is one year of observed change carried forward, and five years of compounding on one year of data flatters the result.

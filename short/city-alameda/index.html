Alameda: the October 2026 read

Alameda has not been a peninsula since 1902. A tidal canal cut through the neck that connected it to Oakland, and the city has treated being an island as its governing fact ever since.

That shows up most clearly in the charter. In 1973 voters added Article 26 and banned new multifamily construction outright. In 1991 a second measure capped residential density citywide at one housing unit per 2,000 square feet of land. A repeal went to the voters in November 2020 as Measure Z and failed. State housing law has since created some room, and the clearest example is a project of roughly 1,200 units on the former naval air station. Anyone buying here should confirm the current rules with the city, because almost nothing about Alameda's housing supply makes sense without them.

The history behind the housing is unusually legible. Downtown exists because the San Francisco and Alameda Railroad stopped at what is now Lincoln and Park in 1864, and the National Register listed the Park Street Historic Commercial District in May 1982. The Gold Coast filled in with Victorians and Edwardians, with a second wave after the electric streetcar arrived in 1892. Neptune Beach ran at the west end from 1917 to 1939 and people called it the Coney Island of the West. Naval Air Station Alameda was commissioned on November 1, 1940, closed in April 1997, and left 1,560 acres that became a National Historic Landmark District in January 2013. South Shore and Bay Farm are younger than all of it because they were dredged out of the bay, starting when the dredge Franciscan arrived on November 7, 1955.

So Alameda is really three housing markets. Pre war stock on the original island, where the question is the condition of a hundred year old house. Mid century and later on engineered fill, where the question is settlement and flood insurance. And new construction at the Point, where the question is the environmental record and the assessments.

Figure out which of the three you are shopping in before you compare prices, because the right inspection, the right insurance question and the right risk are different in each one.

Inside it

This report covers 14 areas of Alameda. What each one actually is:

  • Alameda Point. This is 1,560 acres of the island that belonged to the United States Navy until 1997, and the Secretary of the Interior made the heart of it a National Historic Landmark District in January 2013.
  • Ballena Bay. Ballena Bay is an island off the side of an island, and a good part of it runs on a lease from the city.
  • Bay Farm. A man named Amos Mecartney tried to farm this ground in the 1870s and it did not work out.
  • Bronze Coast. The Bronze Coast sits between the Gold Coast and the rest of the island, and the name tells you exactly what it is: the same era, a step down in scale.
  • Central Alameda. Central Alameda is the part of the island everybody drives through and nobody can draw the boundary of.
  • Fernside. There was a seventy room mansion on Fernside Boulevard and it burned to the ground in 1897 with nobody in it.
  • Gold Coast. The name is not marketing.
  • Harbor Bay Isle. Harbor Bay Isle was supposed to be ten thousand homes.
  • Harbor Bay. Harbor Bay is the half of the Bay Farm fill that was built for work instead of for living, and the ferry is the reason anyone commutes from it.
  • Marina Village. Bethlehem Steel built ships on this ground from 1916 to 1948.
  • Park Street District. The reason downtown Alameda is here is that a train stopped here in 1864.
  • South Shore. This neighborhood did not exist before November 1955.
  • Webster Street District. Webster Street is the West End's main street, and it has had two completely different lives.
  • West End. For twenty two years there was an amusement park at the end of this neighborhood, and people called it the Coney Island of the West.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,145,869 here, transfer tax runs about $15,011 at 13.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $7,505 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $17,188.

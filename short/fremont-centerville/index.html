Centerville, Fremont: the October 2026 read

There is a cemetery at Post Street and Bonde Way that is older than the city around it by a century.

Centerville Pioneer Cemetery goes back to 1855, when the Alameda Presbyterian Church, organized two years earlier, bought the ground under the Reverend William Wallace Brier and nine other founding members. California recognized it as a state cemetery in 1859 and it went into the California Register of Historic Resources in 1976. The names in it are the names on the road signs. Herman Eggers is buried there. So is Blacow, and Blacow Road still runs through this part of Fremont.

That is the useful thing to understand about Centerville. It was one of five separate townships, with Mission San Jose, Niles, Irvington and Warm Springs, and all five voted to become one city on January 23, 1956. Washington Township had organized the area as far back as 1853. Newark was invited into the merger and its voters said no. Newark is a separate city today and incorporated on its own in 1955. Centerville was a town with a main street before Fremont existed, and the commercial spine along Fremont Boulevard and Peralta Boulevard is the remains of it.

What that means on the ground. The housing here is older and denser than the tract neighborhoods east of it, with a mix that includes genuine pre war stock, post war infill and apartment buildings along the arterials. It is also the part of Fremont closest to the Centerville train station. For anyone commuting south that is a real factor.

If you are looking here, do two things. Pull the year built on every address you are serious about, because the range on a single street can be fifty years and the inspection list is completely different at either end. Then go stand at the Centerville station during the evening commute. Rail access is most of why people pay to be on this side of Fremont, and whether the schedule actually fits your day is something you can settle in one trip instead of after you move.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Fremont I cover, and what each one is:

  • Ardenwood. A Pennsylvania farmer named George Washington Patterson came to California for gold in 1849, found almost none, and in 1857 built a redwood farmhouse here instead.
  • Baylands. This is the edge of Fremont where the city stops and the bay starts, and most of it is federally protected.
  • Brookvale. Brookvale is one of the Fremont neighborhoods where the published history is genuinely thin, so this is the development record and the practical read, not a story somebody made up.
  • Cabrillo. Cabrillo is a 1960s tract neighborhood in north Fremont, and the useful facts about it are in the development record instead of in any story.
  • Cherry Guardino. Cherry-Guardino takes its name from its two main streets, and like several Fremont tract neighborhoods it has almost no published history.
  • City Center. On September 6, 1960 the Fremont City Council picked the Fudenna ranch as the site for its civic center.
  • Glenmoor. Glenmoor has a homeowners association that was incorporated in March 1953, when there were no more than 75 houses standing in the whole subdivision.
  • Grimmer. Grimmer is named for a family nobody has documented properly, and the honest thing is to say so.

What it costs to own here

Figure about $8,931 a month to own the typical home in Centerville, $107,170 a year. Loan, property tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance are in that figure. HOA dues are not, and they vary too much to average.

Rates sit at 7.4 percent on the 30-year fixed as of 2026-10-15. A year earlier the same survey read 6.27 percent. Alameda County's 2026 one-unit loan limit sits at $1,249,125. That is the cap for conforming conventional and for FHA alike.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Figure $1,024 in transfer tax on a $930,526 sale here, at 1.10 dollars per thousand. Local practice splits it down the middle, roughly $512 each, though the contract decides.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $13,958.

What waiting costs

On the same trailing rate, two years of waiting adds up to roughly -$61,958 in price and payment combined, and five years to -$179,032.

That rate is one observed year extrapolated forward, not a forecast, and compounding a single strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. Weight the two year figure more heavily.

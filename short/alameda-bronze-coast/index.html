Bronze Coast, Alameda: the October 2026 read

The Bronze Coast sits between the Gold Coast and the rest of the island, and the name tells you exactly what it is: the same era, a step down in scale.

The housing here is early twentieth century, mostly Craftsman, bungalow and Colonial Revival, on compact blocks you can walk end to end. Smaller houses, smaller lots, tighter streets than the blocks immediately south.

Then 1940 happened, and it changed this neighborhood more than almost anywhere else on the island. When the naval air station opened at the west end, Alameda had to house a military workforce in a hurry. Larger homes in this part of the city were cut into small multi unit buildings, and in other cases the original house came down and an apartment building went up in its place. That is the single most important fact about the Bronze Coast if you are buying here, because it means the building type on a given parcel often has nothing to do with what the rest of the block looks like.

It also runs straight into Alameda's zoning history. Voters put Article 26 into the city charter in 1973 and banned new multifamily construction outright. A second measure in 1991 capped density citywide at one unit per 2,000 square feet of land. A repeal went on the 2020 ballot and failed. The practical effect is that many of the multi unit buildings here predate the ban, so they exist legally but could not be built again today.

What to do with that. If you are looking at a duplex, triplex or fourplex here, the legal status of the units is the first thing to establish, not the last. Get the permit history and confirm with the city that every unit is legal and not an unpermitted conversion from the 1940s. Ask whether anything is nonconforming, because a nonconforming building has real limits on what you can rebuild after a loss, and that affects both financing and insurance. Then look at a single family house and a converted one on the same street in the same afternoon. The price difference will tell you what the market here actually pays for.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Alameda I cover, and what each one is:

  • Alameda Point. This is 1,560 acres of the island that belonged to the United States Navy until 1997, and the Secretary of the Interior made the heart of it a National Historic Landmark District in January 2013.
  • Ballena Bay. Ballena Bay is an island off the side of an island, and a good part of it runs on a lease from the city.
  • Bay Farm. A man named Amos Mecartney tried to farm this ground in the 1870s and it did not work out.
  • Central Alameda. Central Alameda is the part of the island everybody drives through and nobody can draw the boundary of.
  • Fernside. There was a seventy room mansion on Fernside Boulevard and it burned to the ground in 1897 with nobody in it.
  • Gold Coast. The name is not marketing.
  • Harbor Bay Isle. Harbor Bay Isle was supposed to be ten thousand homes.
  • Harbor Bay. Harbor Bay is the half of the Bay Farm fill that was built for work instead of for living, and the ferry is the reason anyone commutes from it.

What it costs to own here

Figure about $12,225 a month to own the typical home in Bronze Coast, $146,703 a year. Loan, property tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance are in that figure. HOA dues are not, and they vary too much to average.

Rates sit at 7.4 percent on the 30-year fixed as of 2026-10-15. A year earlier the same survey read 6.27 percent. For 2026, one-unit financing in Alameda County tops out at $1,249,125 on both the conforming and the FHA side.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Transfer tax on the typical Bronze Coast home, $1,309,272, comes to roughly $17,151 at a rate of 13.10 per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $8,576 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $19,639.

What waiting costs

The two year cost of waiting, price plus payment, comes to about $172,352 here. The five year figure is $565,285.

This rests on one observed year carried forward. The two year figure is the defensible one; the five year figure is arithmetic, not a forecast.

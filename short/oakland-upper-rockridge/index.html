Upper Rockridge, Oakland: the October 2026 read

Upper Rockridge burned in 1991 and what stands here now is largely what replaced it.

On the morning of Sunday, October 20, 1991 a grass fire that had been knocked down the day before near the Caldecott Tunnel came back to life shortly before eleven. Winds gusting to 65 miles an hour drove it down the south ridge of Claremont Canyon, and inside thirty minutes it had jumped Highway 24 and Highway 13 and set hundreds of houses alight. By the time it was fully controlled on October 23 it had killed 25 people, injured 150, and destroyed 2,843 single family homes and 437 apartment and condominium units. Losses ran to about 1.5 billion dollars. Cal Fire lists it as the Tunnel Fire and the cause was never determined. Upper Rockridge was one of the neighborhoods the fire reached after it crossed the freeways, and hundreds of houses here went.

That makes this a different market from the Rockridge flats below it, even though the names are nearly the same. Down on College Avenue the housing is pre war Craftsman with knob and tube wiring and clay sewer laterals. Up here a large share of the stock was built after 1991, to post fire codes, on larger hillside lots with bay views. Across Oakland 46.6 percent of the 191,713 housing units predate 1950. On these streets that share is small.

The Rockridge BART station at 5660 College Avenue is still the reason people pay to be in this part of the city, and it is a drive or a steep walk from up here instead of a stroll.

So the work splits in two depending on the year built, and that number is the first thing to pull.

On anything from the rebuild: confirm the construction year, and ask about the roof, the mechanical systems and the windows, all now thirty years old.

On any pre fire survivor: foundation bolting, knob and tube wiring, galvanized supply lines, and the sewer lateral Oakland certifies at sale.

Either way, fire insurance comes before the house. Get a written quote on the exact address with the risk rating explained, ask what defensible space the parcel must maintain and whether it complies, and ask your broker which carriers will write in this zone at all.

Then the hillside list: soils and drainage history, retaining structures and their ownership, and where water leaves the property in a wet month.

What the market is doing right now

Typical home value in Upper Rockridge is $1,887,410 as of 2026-09-30, up 1.18 percent from the prior month and up 7.3 percent over the trailing year. Month over month is the number worth watching in a monthly series. A trailing twelve month figure keeps reporting last winter's market long after it has turned.

Listing activity in ZIP 94618 for 2026-09. Active inventory is 21, up 90.9 percent from the prior month and down 40.8 percent from a year earlier. 18 new listings came on, up 12.5 percent month over month, and 5 went pending. Median days on market is 19, up 18.8 percent month over month and down 15.6 percent year over year.

Median asking price is $1,179,250 at $634 per square foot, on a median listing of 1,889 square feet. Typical rent in the same area is $2,987, up 3.9 percent year over year.

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in Upper Rockridge is up 7.3 percent year over year. The median asking price of what is actively listed in ZIP 94618 is down 12.3 percent year over year, and price per square foot is down 10.9 percent.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

What it costs to own here, month to month

Sellers underestimate this number, consistently, and it is the number that should set the pricing decision. Owning the typical home in Upper Rockridge runs about $612,216 a month, or $7,346,594 a year. That figure is the loan payment at 3.5 percent down and 400 percent, property tax at 1.71 percent, insurance at the California average, utilities for a home of roughly 1,800 square feet, and maintenance budgeted at 1 percent of value a year. HOA dues are not included, because they vary by building and subdivision.

The 15-year average is 400 percent. These are the Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey averages, published weekly, and they are survey averages and not a quote.

On a loan of $1,821,350, the typical home here with the minimum down payment, that year of rate movement is the difference between a payment of $607,117 and $607,117, a change of $0 a month on the same house at the same price. Rate movement has been the larger force on affordability here than price movement.

This is the section most buyers in Upper Rockridge find out about too late. The 2026 one-unit loan limit in Alameda County is $1,249,125. That single figure is both the conforming conventional ceiling and the FHA ceiling. Above it, a loan is a jumbo, underwritten to a different and generally stricter standard, with its own reserve and credit requirements and no FHA option at all.

At the typical value here of $1,887,410, a 3.5 percent down payment leaves a loan of $1,821,350. That is $572,225 over the ceiling. To bring the loan down to the limit on a home at this value, the down payment has to be $638,285, or 33.8 percent. Put differently, the most expensive home a minimum-down FHA loan reaches in Alameda County is about $1,294,430, and the typical home in Upper Rockridge is $592,980 above that.

There are three honest paths from here, and they are worth knowing before touring anything. Bring the larger down payment and stay conforming. Take a jumbo and qualify under its standards. Or shop at or below $1,294,430. In Oakland that means looking at the neighborhoods further down the ranking table above, where the minimum-down programs still function. Each of those is a real strategy. What does not work is assuming the 3.5 percent down program applies at this price.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,887,410 here, transfer tax runs about $30,387 at 16.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $15,194 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $2,000,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 18.60 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $5,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $2,000,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Emeryville at 26.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $47,185.

What waiting actually costs here

This is the math on waiting, and the assumptions are stated, not buried. Appreciation is set at 7.3 percent, this neighborhood's own trailing twelve month change in typical value as of 2026-09-30. The rate is held at the current 400 percent average. The down payment is 3.5 percent. The formula is the price increase plus the payment increase over the waiting period, with rent shown separately and not folded into the headline figure.

At those assumptions, a home at $1,887,410 today becomes $2,171,131 in two years and $2,678,650 in five. The monthly payment moves from $607,117 to $698,381 and then $861,633. The cost of waiting comes to $2,474,054 over two years and $16,062,184 over five. Rent over those periods adds $71,696 and $179,240 on top, at a typical area rent of $2,987.

Now the part that most versions of this calculation leave out. Extrapolating 7.3 percent for five years is an extrapolation of a single observed year, not a forecast, and compounding one strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. It is worth seeing what the same math does on a more conservative input. At the citywide rate of 0.6 percent instead, the same home reaches $1,908,378 in two years and $1,940,267 in five. That is $738,383 less than the neighborhood-rate projection.

The cost of waiting is also not one-directional. Rates move, and they have moved more than prices here over the past year. Hold the price constant and move only the rate and the rate component shows up on its own. If rates fall, part of the cost of waiting reverses. If they rise, it compounds. Anyone presenting this calculation as a single certain number is selling, not calculating.

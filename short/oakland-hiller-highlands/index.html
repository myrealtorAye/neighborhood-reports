Hiller Highlands, Oakland: the October 2026 read

Hiller Highlands was the first place the 1991 firestorm hit hard, and it is where the first people died.

On the morning of Sunday, October 20, 1991 a grass fire that had been knocked down the day before near the Caldecott Tunnel came back to life shortly before eleven. Winds gusting to 65 miles an hour drove it down the south ridge of Claremont Canyon, and inside thirty minutes it had jumped Highway 24 and Highway 13 and set hundreds of houses alight. By the time it was fully controlled on October 23 it had killed 25 people, injured 150, and destroyed 2,843 single family homes and 437 apartment and condominium units. Losses ran to about 1.5 billion dollars. Cal Fire lists it as the Tunnel Fire and the cause was never determined.

This neighborhood took the most concentrated damage of anywhere. Residents put up the Firestorm Memorial Garden here, and it is the reason the housing stock on these streets is almost entirely post 1991 construction.

That is the single most useful thing to know about buying in Hiller Highlands. In a city where 46.6 percent of the 191,713 housing units predate 1950, these blocks are newer than almost anywhere in Oakland, built to post fire codes, on a hillside with bay and bridge views.

So the pre war inspection list does not apply here. A different and more expensive one does.

Fire insurance comes first, before the house. Get a written quote on the exact address before your contingencies expire and ask the broker to explain the risk rating and what the carrier requires. In the Oakland hills this is not a closing detail, it is a monthly number that can decide whether you can afford the house at all, and some carriers will not write here at all.

Ask what defensible space the property is required to maintain and whether it currently complies, because that is both an insurance condition and a city requirement.

Then the hillside list: soils and drainage history for the parcel, who owns and maintains any retaining structures and what condition they are in, and where water leaves the property in a wet month.

On the house itself, confirm the year built and ask whether it was part of the post fire rebuild, because the rebuild cohort was constructed to a specific code moment and that is useful to know.

Then drive the route out at the hour you would leave. These are narrow switchback streets and in 1991 that mattered.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Oakland I cover, and what each one is:

  • Acorn. Fifty blocks of this neighborhood were demolished.
  • Adams Point. Adams Point is named for Edson Adams, one of the men who got hold of Oakland's land early, and in 1906 it filled up with San Franciscans who had nowhere else to go.
  • Allendale. The Allendale tract map was recorded in 1892, and there was a cinema operating here by 1913.
  • Brookfield Village. Twelve hundred houses went up here during the Second World War and they sold for four thousand dollars each.
  • Bushrod. Bushrod was annexed by Oakland in 1897, in the same batch as Temescal, Golden Gate and Piedmont Avenue, and that date is the most solid thing in its published record.
  • Caballo Hills. Caballo Hills is a hillside pocket at the southeastern edge of Oakland with very little published history, so this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Castlemont. Castlemont takes its name from the high school at its centre, and its published history is thin enough that this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Chabot Park. Anthony Chabot dammed a creek in 1868 and built Oakland its first water supply at municipal scale, and his name is on the parkland at the edge of this neighborhood.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,264,131 here, transfer tax runs about $20,353 at 16.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $10,176 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $2,000,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 18.60 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $5,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $2,000,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $18,962.

Webster Street District, Alameda: the October 2026 read

Webster Street is the West End's main street, and it has had two completely different lives.

In the 1950s it was a Navy town commercial strip: sailors, bars, all night restaurants, drive in theaters. The naval air station had been commissioned on November 1, 1940 at the end of the island, and Webster Street served it for fifty seven years. The base closed in April 1997. The customers left in one stroke. A merchant who lived through it summed it up by saying the closure was good for Park Street and it killed Webster Street. Local writing from the period describes Webster becoming a line many islanders chose not to cross.

That was almost thirty years ago, and the rebuilding has been the story of this corridor ever since. Alameda Point, the 1,560 acres the Navy left behind, is immediately west, and it is the one part of the city where significant new housing is actually going up, including a project of roughly 1,200 units. Everything new at the Point arrives through this end of town.

So Webster Street is the corridor with the most to gain from what happens at Alameda Point, and that is the honest investment case for being near it.

Treat it as exactly that, with the risk stated. Walk the street yourself, on a weekday and on a weekend, and count what is open and what is empty. That count is better information than any projection. Then on the housing: the West End stock is older and denser and a great deal of it was converted to flats during the Navy years, so get the permit history, confirm the legal unit count with the city, and ask whether the building is nonconforming under the charter density rules that voters put in place in 1973 and tightened in 1991. Nonconforming status changes what you can rebuild and it changes your insurance. Find out before you are in contract, not after.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Alameda I cover, and what each one is:

  • Alameda Point. This is 1,560 acres of the island that belonged to the United States Navy until 1997, and the Secretary of the Interior made the heart of it a National Historic Landmark District in January 2013.
  • Ballena Bay. Ballena Bay is an island off the side of an island, and a good part of it runs on a lease from the city.
  • Bay Farm. A man named Amos Mecartney tried to farm this ground in the 1870s and it did not work out.
  • Bronze Coast. The Bronze Coast sits between the Gold Coast and the rest of the island, and the name tells you exactly what it is: the same era, a step down in scale.
  • Central Alameda. Central Alameda is the part of the island everybody drives through and nobody can draw the boundary of.
  • Fernside. There was a seventy room mansion on Fernside Boulevard and it burned to the ground in 1897 with nobody in it.
  • Gold Coast. The name is not marketing.
  • Harbor Bay Isle. Harbor Bay Isle was supposed to be ten thousand homes.

What it costs to own here

Put the minimum down on the typical Webster Street District home and the monthly carry lands near $10,804. That is $129,647 across a year, counting loan, tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance. HOA dues are not in it, since no two are alike.

The 30-year fixed average is 7.4 percent as of 2026-10-15, against 6.27 percent in the same week last year. For 2026, one-unit financing in Alameda County tops out at $1,249,125 on both the conforming and the FHA side.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

On a home at $1,145,869 here, transfer tax runs about $15,011 at 13.10 dollars per thousand. Custom is to split it, so about $7,505 a side, and who pays is set by the contract.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $17,188.

What waiting costs

Price and payment together, a two year wait here runs about $56,488. Stretch it to five and the figure is $175,964.

This rests on one observed year carried forward. The two year figure is the defensible one; the five year figure is arithmetic, not a forecast.

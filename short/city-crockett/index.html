Crockett: the October 2026 read

61.4 percent of Crockett's housing was built before 1950, and down the strait in Port Costa the figure is 91.4 percent.

Crockett is a sugar town. The C and H refinery on the Carquinez Strait is the reason the place exists and it still dominates the waterfront. American Sugar Refining bought C and H in 2006, and the Crockett plant processed its last shipment of Hawaiian sugar in 2017. Port Costa below it was a wheat shipping port before the sugar era. Both are unincorporated Contra Costa County territory.

Crockett has 1,713 housing units, 61.4 percent of them pre 1950, 68.0 percent detached, 56.5 percent owner occupied, with an ACS median value of 776,100 dollars and ZHVI about 659,000, down 1.2 percent over the year. Port Costa has 210 units, 91.4 percent of them pre 1950 and 100.0 percent detached, 56.2 percent owner occupied, ACS median value 600,000 dollars and ZHVI about 659,000, up 2.2 percent.

So these are the oldest housing markets in the county and the questions are the pre war questions, every one of them. Foundation bolting. Knob and tube wiring. Galvanized supply lines. Original windows and siding. The legal unit count from the county, not the listing. The permit history. Whether the building is nonconforming.

Then confirm the parcel is unincorporated, pull the zoning for it and its neighbours, and ask for the site history in writing, because both towns sit in a working industrial corridor. With 210 units, Port Costa will not give you a useful monthly comparable sample, so plan to reason from a handful of closed sales.

One thing worth spelling out, because it catches buyers. In an unincorporated place there is no city hall standing between you and the county. Your building permits and inspections come from the county, code enforcement is the county's, the Sheriff answers calls unless a special district provides policing, and fire, water and sewer frequently come from separate districts that each levy on the parcel. So get the full list of districts for the address off the county's tax roll, not the listing, and read what each one charges. Then ask the county's Local Agency Formation Commission whether the parcel sits in any city's sphere of influence, because that is where future annexation would start and it is a public record.

And one habit worth keeping in this county. Pull the year built before you form any opinion on a listing, because it decides the entire inspection list and it is the single most useful number on a property profile. Then get the tax bill for the exact address off the county roll, not the listing estimate, and read which districts and assessments are on it. Those two documents cost nothing, they take a day, and between them they settle most of what a buyer actually needs to know before writing an offer.

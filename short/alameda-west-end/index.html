West End, Alameda: the October 2026 read

For twenty two years there was an amusement park at the end of this neighborhood, and people called it the Coney Island of the West.

Robert Strehlow opened Neptune Beach in 1917 on the waterfront that is now Crab Cove. Admission was a dime. There was a pool over three hundred feet long, a roller coaster, a hand carved carousel, barbecue pits, a clubhouse for dances, and high diving and steeplechase events. Ferries and the Southern Pacific brought the crowds in. The Depression, the new Bay Bridge and the shift to cars took the crowds away again, and the park closed in 1939. In 1940 they auctioned off the ferris wheel, the roller coaster and the carousel.

The year the park's rides were sold off, the Navy arrived. Naval Air Station Alameda was commissioned on November 1, 1940 at the west end of the island, and for the next fifty seven years the West End was a Navy neighborhood. Webster Street was its commercial spine. In the 1950s it ran to sailors, bars, all night restaurants and drive in theaters. The base closed in April 1997 and Webster Street lost its customer base in a single stroke. One longtime merchant put it plainly: the closure was good for Park Street and it killed Webster Street.

That history is still priced into this part of the island. The West End has the island's older and denser housing, more multi unit buildings, and a commercial street that has been rebuilding for almost thirty years.

What to do about it as a buyer. Check the year built and then check whether the building is one unit or several, because a lot of large single family houses on this side of Alameda were cut into flats during the Navy years and the conversion quality varies enormously. Pull the permit history on anything that looks converted. Then walk Webster Street on a weekday and again on a Saturday. The direction this corridor is moving is the single biggest variable in what a West End house is worth in ten years, and you can read it better standing on the sidewalk than in a spreadsheet.

What the market is doing right now

Typical home value in West End is $1,036,847 as of 2026-09-30, up 0.47 percent from the prior month and down 1.2 percent over the trailing year. Month over month is the number worth watching in a monthly series. A trailing twelve month figure keeps reporting last winter's market long after it has turned.

Listing activity in Alameda for 2026-09. Active inventory is 71. 42 new listings came on, and 29 went pending. Median days on market is 34.

Median asking price is $972,000 at $617 per square foot.

Two numbers in this report move in opposite directions. It is worth saying why, and not quietly picking the flattering one. Typical home value in West End is down 1.2 percent year over year. Realtor.com does not publish year over year listing figures at this geography, so the comparison below rests on the value index and the current month's listings.

They disagree because they measure different populations. The Zillow Home Value Index estimates the typical value of every home in the area, listed or not, and it is smoothed and seasonally adjusted. The median listing price describes only the homes that happen to be on the market this month. Change the mix of what is listed, and the median asking price moves without any individual home changing value. A month heavier in condominiums and smaller units pulls the median asking price down even while the typical value of the housing stock rises.

The practical read: use the value index to understand what the housing stock is worth, and use the listing figures to understand the conditions you are transacting in right now. For a seller, the listing numbers are the ones that describe the competition. For an owner estimating equity, the value index is the better guide. Neither one is the market on its own.

What waiting actually costs here

This is the math on waiting, and the assumptions are stated, not buried. Appreciation is set at -1.2 percent, this neighborhood's own trailing twelve month change in typical value as of 2026-09-30. The rate is held at the current 400 percent average. The down payment is 3.5 percent. The formula is the price increase plus the payment increase over the waiting period, with rent shown separately and not folded into the headline figure.

At those assumptions, a home at $1,036,847 today becomes $1,012,069 in two years and $976,007 in five. The monthly payment moves from $333,519 to $325,549 and then $313,949. The cost of waiting comes to -$216,067 over two years and -$1,235,052 over five.

Now the part that most versions of this calculation leave out. Extrapolating -1.2 percent for five years is an extrapolation of a single observed year, not a forecast, and compounding one strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. It is worth seeing what the same math does on a more conservative input. At the citywide rate of 2.1 percent instead, the same home reaches $1,080,897 in two years and $1,150,505 in five. That is -$174,498 less than the neighborhood-rate projection.

The cost of waiting is also not one-directional. Rates move, and they have moved more than prices here over the past year. Hold the price constant and move only the rate and the rate component shows up on its own. If rates fall, part of the cost of waiting reverses. If they rise, it compounds. Anyone presenting this calculation as a single certain number is selling, not calculating.

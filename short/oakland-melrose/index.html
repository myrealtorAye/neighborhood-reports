Melrose, Oakland: the October 2026 read

Melrose was its own town until Oakland absorbed it in 1909.

That annexation was the largest in Oakland's history. It took in Melrose, Elmhurst, Fruitvale and Fitchburg in one move, carried the city from 22.9 square miles to roughly 60, and came on the back of a population that doubled to 150,174 by 1910, most of the increase from people who crossed the bay after the 1906 earthquake. Before that, Melrose was a separate community along the rail corridor through East Oakland, and the alignment of the tracks is still the reason the street grid breaks where it does.

Past the annexation the published history is thin, so the rest is the development record and the practical read.

The housing is East Oakland flats stock: Victorian and early twentieth century houses, a good deal of it divided into flats over the decades, with apartment buildings and commercial frontage along the arterials. Oakland has 191,713 housing units. 71,234 were built in 1939 or earlier and 18,124 between 1940 and 1949, putting 46.6 percent of the city before 1950. 41.5 percent of units are single family detached, owner occupancy is 42.3 percent, the ACS median value is 929,900 dollars and median gross rent is 1,979.

So the legal unit count is the first question, ahead of condition and ahead of price.

Confirm it with the city, not from the listing. Get the permit history. Ask whether the building is nonconforming, because that limits what you can rebuild after a loss and affects both financing and insurance. If it holds more than one unit, read Oakland's rent adjustment program and the just cause for eviction rules before you model a dollar of income.

Then the pre war list in full: foundation bolting, knob and tube wiring, galvanized supply lines, the sewer lateral Oakland certifies at sale, and the condition of original windows and siding.

On any parcel near the rail corridor, ask for the site history in writing, because industrial uses followed the tracks through here for a century.

Inside it

For context, the other parts of Oakland I cover, and what each one is:

  • Acorn. Fifty blocks of this neighborhood were demolished.
  • Adams Point. Adams Point is named for Edson Adams, one of the men who got hold of Oakland's land early, and in 1906 it filled up with San Franciscans who had nowhere else to go.
  • Allendale. The Allendale tract map was recorded in 1892, and there was a cinema operating here by 1913.
  • Brookfield Village. Twelve hundred houses went up here during the Second World War and they sold for four thousand dollars each.
  • Bushrod. Bushrod was annexed by Oakland in 1897, in the same batch as Temescal, Golden Gate and Piedmont Avenue, and that date is the most solid thing in its published record.
  • Caballo Hills. Caballo Hills is a hillside pocket at the southeastern edge of Oakland with very little published history, so this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Castlemont. Castlemont takes its name from the high school at its centre, and its published history is thin enough that this is the development record and the buying read.
  • Chabot Park. Anthony Chabot dammed a creek in 1868 and built Oakland its first water supply at municipal scale, and his name is on the parkland at the edge of this neighborhood.

What it costs to own here

Put the minimum down on the typical Melrose home and the monthly carry lands near $4,938. That is $59,253 across a year, counting loan, tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance. HOA dues are not in it, since no two are alike.

Rates sit at 7.4 percent on the 30-year fixed as of 2026-10-15. A year earlier the same survey read 6.27 percent. In Alameda County the 2026 one-unit limit is $1,249,125. It serves as the conforming conventional ceiling and the FHA ceiling at once.

The transfer tax line that costs real money

Melrose charges 16.10 per thousand, so the typical $458,707 home owes roughly $7,385 at transfer. Expect it split, near enough $3,693 apiece, with the contract having the final say.

The part that costs people money is the bracket. The rate that applies is charged on the ENTIRE sale price, not only the amount above the line. At $2,000,000 the rate steps from 16.10 to 18.60 per thousand, so a sale one dollar over that line pays about $5,000 more than a sale one dollar under it. On anything pricing near $2,000,000, the number to run is not the percentage. It is which side of the line the contract price lands on.

And it is set by the city, not the county. Dublin charges 1.10 per thousand against Oakland at 16.10. Same county, same price, and the spread is $6,881.

What waiting costs

Hold off two years and the combined price and payment cost is near $9,906. At five years it reaches $30,317.

That rate is one observed year extrapolated forward, not a forecast, and compounding a single strong year out to five produces a large number by construction. Weight the two year figure more heavily.
